# BDS 1st Year — Pathology Quick Revision Cheatsheet
*Based on Kathmandu University Past Questions (2015–2022)*

This cheatsheet distills the most frequently tested concepts, diagnostic laboratory criteria, cellular mechanisms, and pathological classifications from the extracted past papers. Use it for rapid review before your exams.

## 1. General Pathology: Cell Injury, Cell Death & Adaptations

### Reversible vs. Irreversible Cell Injury
* **Reversible Injury:** Morphological and functional changes that resolve when the damaging stimulus is removed.
  * *Hallmarks:* Cellular swelling (**hydropic change / vacuolar degeneration**) due to failure of ATP-dependent $Na^+/K^+$ pumps; fatty change (steatosis) in hepatocytes; plasma membrane blebbing; loss of microvilli; mitochondrial swelling; ribosome detachment from rough endoplasmic reticulum (RER).
* **Irreversible Injury (Point of No Return):** Persistent or severe injury leading invariably to cell death (necrosis or apoptosis).
  * *Hallmarks:* **Massive calcium influx** into the cytosol; severe structural and functional mitochondrial damage with large, flocculent amorphous densities; widespread rupture of lysosomal membranes releasing autolytic acid hydrolases; profound defects in plasma membrane permeability.

### Necrosis vs. Apoptosis
| Feature | Necrosis | Apoptosis |
|:---|:---|:---|
| **Cell Size** | Enlarged (swelling) | Shrunk, condensed |
| **Nucleus** | Pyknosis → Karyorrhexis → Karyolysis | Fragmentation into nucleosome-sized fragments (180–200 bp ladder) |
| **Plasma Membrane** | Disrupted, leaky | Intact; altered lipid orientation (phosphatidylserine flips to outer leaflet) |
| **Cellular Contents** | Enzymatic digestion; leak into extracellular fluid | Intact; packaged into membrane-bound **apoptotic bodies** |
| **Adjacent Inflammation** | **Present** (frequent tissue destruction) | **Absent** (no inflammatory response) |
| **Physiological / Pathological** | Invariably **pathological** | Often **physiological** (embryogenesis, hormone withdrawal); can be pathological (DNA damage) |
| **Energy Requirement** | Passive (ATP depletion) | Active (requires ATP) |

### Types of Tissue Necrosis
1. **Coagulative Necrosis:**
   * *Mechanism:* Denaturation of both structural proteins and enzymatic proteins blocks proteolysis; **basic cellular architecture ("tombstone outline") is preserved for several days**.
   * *Occurrence:* Characteristic of **ischemic infarction in all solid organs EXCEPT the brain** (e.g. Myocardial Infarction, renal infarct, splenic infarct).
2. **Liquefactive Necrosis:**
   * *Mechanism:* Rapid enzymatic digestion of dead cells transforms tissue into a liquid, viscous mass.
   * *Occurrence:* Characteristic of **hypoxic / ischemic necrosis in the Central Nervous System (brain infarct)**; also seen in acute focal bacterial and fungal infections where neutrophils release powerful hydrolytic enzymes (**abscess formation**).
3. **Caseous Necrosis:**
   * *Morphology:* Friable, yellow-white, cheese-like gross appearance. Microscopically: complete loss of tissue architecture, replaced by structureless, amorphous, eosinophilic granular debris surrounded by a granulomatous inflammatory rim.
   * *Occurrence:* Pathognomonic of **Tuberculosis (TB)** and certain fungal infections.
4. **Fat Necrosis:**
   * *Enzymatic Fat Necrosis:* In acute pancreatitis, activated pancreatic lipases liquefy adipocyte membranes; released fatty acids combine with calcium to form insoluble chalky-white calcium soaps (**saponification**).
   * *Traumatic Fat Necrosis:* Follows mechanical trauma to adipose tissue (e.g. female breast).
5. **Fibrinoid Necrosis:**
   * Deposition of immune complexes (antigen-antibody) combined with fibrin leaking from damaged vessel walls; produces a bright pink, amorphous appearance on H&E staining.
   * *Occurrence:* Immune-mediated vasculitis (Polyarteritis nodosa), Malignant hypertension, Aschoff nodules in rheumatic carditis.
6. **Gangrenous Necrosis:**
   * *Dry Gangrene:* Coagulative necrosis resulting from chronic arterial occlusion of limbs; tissues become dry, shriveled, and blackened with a clear line of demarcation.
   * *Wet Gangrene:* Superimposed bacterial liquefactive infection over ischemic tissue; moist, foul-smelling, rapidly spreading without a sharp line of demarcation (e.g. diabetic foot, acute appendicitis).

### Mechanism of Apoptosis
* **Intrinsic (Mitochondrial) Pathway (Major in physiological and stress conditions):**
  * Triggered by DNA damage, p53 activation, growth factor withdrawal, or ER stress.
  * Pro-apoptotic members of the Bcl-2 family (**Bax and Bak**) oligomerize and insert into the outer mitochondrial membrane, forming pores (**Mitochondrial Outer Membrane Permeabilization - MOMP**).
  * **Cytochrome c** leaks from the intermembrane space into the cytosol and binds **Apaf-1** (Apoptotic protease activating factor-1) in the presence of dATP/ATP to form the wheel-shaped **Apoptosome**.
  * The apoptosome recruits and cleaves procaspase-9 into active **initiator Caspase-9**.
  * Anti-apoptotic members (**Bcl-2, Bcl-xL**) normally prevent Bax/Bak pore formation.
* **Extrinsic (Death Receptor-Initiated) Pathway:**
  * Triggered by engagement of plasma membrane death receptors (members of the TNF-receptor superfamily containing an intracellular "death domain"): **Fas (CD95)** and **TNFR1**.
  * FasL (on cytotoxic T cells) binds Fas → recruits cytoplasmic adapter protein **FADD** (Fas-associated death domain) → cleaves procaspase-8 into active **initiator Caspase-8** (or Caspase-10).
* **Execution Phase (Common Pathway):**
  * Initiator caspases (8 and 9) cleave and activate **executioner caspases: Caspase-3, Caspase-6, and Caspase-7**.
  * Executioner caspases cleave structural cytoskeletal proteins and activate **CAD (Caspase-Activated DNase)**, causing internucleosomal cleavage of DNA into characteristic fragments of 180–200 base pairs.
  * Phosphatidylserine flips from the inner leaflet to the outer leaflet of the plasma membrane, acting as an **"eat-me" signal** that triggers rapid phagocytosis by macrophages without releasing pro-inflammatory contents.

### Pathological Calcification
* **Dystrophic Calcification:**
  * *Serum Calcium Level:* **Normal**.
  * *Tissue State:* **Dead, dying, degenerated, or necrotic tissues**.
  * *Pathogenesis:* Initiated by calcium binding to phospholipids in membrane vesicles of degenerating cells, followed by phosphate addition forming hydroxyapatite crystals.
  * *Examples:* Atherosclerotic plaques in advanced arterial disease, old healed tuberculous caseous lymph nodes/Ghon focus, calcific aortic stenosis, lithopedion.
* **Metastatic Calcification:**
  * *Serum Calcium Level:* **Elevated (Hypercalcemia)**.
  * *Tissue State:* **Normal, healthy tissues**.
  * *Causes of Hypercalcemia:* Primary hyperparathyroidism (parathyroid adenoma), osteolytic bone metastases, Multiple Myeloma, Vitamin D intoxication, chronic renal failure (secondary hyperparathyroidism).
  * *Preferred Sites:* Interstitial tissues of organs that excrete acid, resulting in a relatively alkaline local internal pH: **gastric mucosa** (secretes HCl), **kidneys** (secretes acid into urine, producing nephrocalcinosis), and **lungs** (excretes CO₂).

## 2. Inflammation & Wound Healing

### Acute Inflammation
* Rapid host defense response to deliver leukocytes and plasma proteins to the site of infection or injury.
* **Vascular Events:**
  1. *Transient Arteriolar Vasoconstriction:* Lasts seconds; neurogenic reflex.
  2. *Arteriolar Vasodilation:* Mediated by **Histamine** and **Nitric Oxide (NO)**. Causes increased local blood flow, resulting in **Erythema / Redness (Rubor)** and **Heat (Calor)**.
  3. *Increased Vascular Permeability:* Primarily affects post-capillary venules. Endothelial cell contraction induced by histamine, bradykinin, and leukotrienes creates inter-endothelial gaps, allowing exudation of protein-rich fluid into tissues, causing **Edema / Swelling (Tumor)**.
  4. *Stasis:* Fluid loss leads to hemoconcentration and slowing of blood flow, facilitating leukocyte margination.
* **Cellular Events (Leukocyte Recruitment Sequence):**
  1. *Margination:* Leukocytes move from the central axial stream toward the periphery of the vessel lumen.
  2. *Rolling:* Weak, transient tethering mediated by **Selectins** (P-selectin and E-selectin on endothelium bind Sialyl-Lewis X on leukocytes; L-selectin on leukocytes).
  3. *Firm Adhesion:* High-affinity binding mediated by **Integrins** on leukocytes (LFA-1, Mac-1 / CD11b/CD18) interacting with endothelial immunoglobulin superfamily molecules (**ICAM-1, VCAM-1**), stimulated by chemokines.
  4. *Transmigration / Diapedesis:* Leukocytes squeeze through endothelial junctions, mediated by **PECAM-1 (CD31)**.
  5. *Chemotaxis:* Directed migration along a chemical gradient toward the injury site. Chemoattractants: **C5a**, **Leukotriene $B_4$ ($LTB_4$)**, bacterial N-formyl peptides, and **Interleukin-8 (IL-8)**.
  6. *Phagocytosis & Intracellular Killing:*
     * *Opsonization:* Target coated with opsonins: **IgG ($Fc$ fragment)** and **C3b**.
     * *Engulfment:* Pseudopodia extend to enclose microbe within a phagosome, which fuses with a lysosome to form a phagolysosome.
     * *Killing (Respiratory Burst):* Membrane-bound **NADPH oxidase** reduces O₂ to superoxide radical ($O_2^{\bullet-}$), converted to $H_2O_2$ by SOD. **Myeloperoxidase (MPO)** from azurophilic granules combines $H_2O_2$ with chloride ($Cl^-$) to generate **hypochlorous acid ($HOCl^{\bullet}$ - bleach)**, which destroys bacterial cell walls.

### Chronic & Granulomatous Inflammation
* **Chronic Inflammation:** Inflammation of prolonged duration (weeks to months) characterized by simultaneous tissue destruction, mononuclear leukocyte infiltration (**macrophages, lymphocytes, plasma cells**), and repair attempts (**angiogenesis and fibrosis**).
* **Granulomatous Inflammation:** A distinctive pattern of chronic inflammation characterized by focal collections of activated macrophages, often with an epithelial-like appearance (**epithelioid cells**), surrounded by a collar of lymphocytes and plasma cells.
  * *Epithelioid Macrophage:* Abundant pale eosinophilic cytoplasm, elongated slipper-shaped vesicular nuclei, indistinct cell borders.
  * *Giant Cells:* Formed by the fusion of multiple activated macrophages:
    * **Langhans Giant Cells:** Nuclei arranged in a **horseshoe-shaped / peripheral horseshoe pattern** at the cell margin (classic for Tuberculosis).
    * **Foreign Body Giant Cells:** Nuclei scattered haphazardly throughout the cytoplasm.

### Wound Healing: Primary vs. Secondary Intention
| Feature | Healing by Primary Intention (First Intention) | Healing by Secondary Intention (Second Intention) |
|:---|:---|:---|
| **Wound Nature** | Clean, uninfected, surgical incised wound with closely approximated margins (e.g. sutured surgical incision) | Large tissue defect, irregular margins, infected, or non-approximated (e.g. extraction socket, large ulcer) |
| **Tissue Loss** | Minimal | Significant |
| **Granulation Tissue** | Minimal amount formed | **Abundant granulation tissue** fills the large defect |
| **Wound Contraction** | Negligible | **Prominent wound contraction** mediated by **myofibroblasts** |
| **Scarring** | Minimal, linear, aesthetically neat scar | Large, irregular, puckered scar; risk of wound contracture |
| **Healing Duration** | Rapid (7–10 days) | Prolonged (weeks to months) |
* **Factors Delaying Wound Healing:**
  * *Local:* **Infection (single most common cause)**, poor blood supply / local ischemia, foreign bodies, excessive mechanical movement, previous radiation.
  * *Systemic:* **Diabetes Mellitus**, nutritional deficiencies (**Vitamin C** required for proline/lysine hydroxylation in collagen; Zinc required for collagenases; Protein deficiency), **Corticosteroids** (inhibit TGF-$\beta$ and fibrosis), advanced age.

## 3. Hemodynamics, Thrombosis & Shock

### Virchow's Triad & Thrombosis
* Three primary abnormalities predisposing to intravascular thrombus formation:
  1. **Endothelial Injury:** Dominant factor in arterial thrombosis. Exposure of subendothelial collagen and vWF triggers platelet adherence and activation (e.g. ulcerated atherosclerotic plaque, vasculitis).
  2. **Abnormal Blood Flow (Stasis or Turbulence):** Turbulence causes endothelial injury and countercurrent pockets; stasis disrupts normal laminar flow (brings platelets into contact with endothelium) and prevents dilution of activated clotting factors (e.g. atrial fibrillation, prolonged immobilization, varicose veins).
  3. **Hypercoagulability (Thrombophilia):** Inherited (Factor V Leiden mutation, Prothrombin gene G20210A mutation, Antithrombin III deficiency) or acquired (malignancy, oral contraceptives, antiphospholipid syndrome).

### Shock & Septic Shock Pathogenesis
* **Definition of Shock:** State of systemic hypoperfusion resulting from decreased effective circulating cardiac output or reduced blood volume, leading to cellular hypoxia and multi-organ metabolic failure.
* **Pathogenesis of Septic Shock:**
  * Most frequently caused by Gram-negative bacterial infections releasing **Lipopolysaccharide (LPS / Endotoxin)** or Gram-positive infections releasing superantigens/peptidoglycans.
  * LPS binds circulating LPS-Binding Protein (LBP), which engages **CD14 and TLR-4 (Toll-Like Receptor 4)** on monocytes, macrophages, and dendritic cells.
  * Massive release of pro-inflammatory cytokines: **Tumor Necrosis Factor-$\alpha$ (TNF-$\alpha$)**, **Interleukin-1 (IL-1)**, and **IL-6**.
  * *Systemic Consequences:*
    1. *Systemic Endothelial Activation:* High-output nitric oxide (iNOS) release causes profound **systemic vasodilation and vascular collapse (hypotension)**.
    2. *Widespread Microvascular Permeability:* Massive transudation of fluid into interstitium leading to systemic edema.
    3. *Procoagulant State (DIC):* Endothelial injury and tissue factor expression trigger microvascular thrombosis and ischemic organ hypoperfusion.
    4. *Metabolic derangements:* Hyperglycemia, insulin resistance, lactic acidosis.
    5. *Multi-Organ Dysfunction Syndrome (MODS):* Acute tubular necrosis (kidneys), ARDS (shock lung), hepatic dysfunction, and cardiovascular collapse.
* **Stages of Shock:**
  1. *Non-Progressive (Compensated) Stage:* Compensatory neurohumoral mechanisms maintain vital organ perfusion (baroreceptor reflexes, catecholamine release, RAAS, ADH, generalized arteriolar constriction, tachycardia).
  2. *Progressive Stage:* Tissue hypoperfusion leads to widespread hypoxia, intracellular anaerobic glycolysis, massive **lactic acidosis**, and arteriolar pooling of blood.
  3. *Irreversible Stage:* Severe, pervasive cellular and membrane damage; lysosomal enzyme leakage; myocardial depression; complete renal shutdown; mortality approaches 100%.

### Disseminated Intravascular Coagulation (DIC)
* Pathological thrombohemorrhagic syndrome characterized by systemic activation of the coagulation cascade, causing widespread microthrombi formation and secondary **consumption of clotting factors and platelets (consumption coagulopathy)**, leading to paradoxically severe hemorrhagic manifestations.
* **Etiology:** Sepsis, obstetric complications (abruptio placentae, amniotic fluid embolism), severe trauma/burns, acute promyelocytic leukemia, extensive carcinomas.
* **Diagnostic Laboratory Findings:**
  * Platelet count: **Markedly decreased (Thrombocytopenia)** (<$100 \times 10^9$/L).
  * Prothrombin Time (PT) and Activated Partial Thromboplastin Time (APTT): **Prolonged**.
  * Plasma Fibrinogen: **Decreased** (consumed).
  * Fibrin Degradation Products (FDPs) and **D-dimer**: **Markedly elevated** (indicates secondary fibrinolysis).
  * Peripheral Blood Smear: **Schistocytes (fragmented RBCs / helmet cells)** resulting from microangiopathic mechanical shearing against fibrin strands in microvessels.

## 4. Neoplasia

### Benign vs. Malignant Tumors
| Feature | Benign Tumors | Malignant Tumors |
|:---|:---|:---|
| **Differentiation / Anaplasia** | Well-differentiated; cells closely resemble normal parent tissue of origin | Variable; frequently display **anaplasia (lack of differentiation)**: cellular and nuclear **pleomorphism**, high Nuclear:Cytoplasmic (N:C) ratio (1:1 instead of normal 1:4), coarse hyperchromatic nuclei, prominent nucleoli, atypical/tripolar mitotic figures, loss of cell polarity |
| **Rate of Growth** | Slow, progressive growth over years | Usually rapid; may be erratic |
| **Local Invasion** | Remains localized; typically **encapsulated or circumscribed**; does not infiltrate adjacent tissues | **Locally invasive and infiltrative**; permeates, destroys, and invades surrounding normal tissues; lacks true capsule |
| **Metastasis** | **Absent (NEVER metastasizes)** | **Present (hallmark of malignancy)**; capability to spread to non-contiguous distant sites |

### Routes of Tumor Metastasis
1. **Lymphatic Spread:**
   * **Most common pathway for the initial dissemination of Carcinomas** (epithelial malignancies; e.g. oral squamous cell carcinoma metastasizing to submandibular and deep cervical lymph nodes; breast carcinoma to axillary lymph nodes).
   * Follows natural lymphatic drainage routes. The **Sentinel Lymph Node** is the first regional lymph node that receives lymphatic drainage from a primary tumor.
2. **Hematogenous Spread:**
   * **Typical of Sarcomas** (mesenchymal malignancies), but also utilized by carcinomas (notably Renal Cell Carcinoma, Hepatocellular Carcinoma, Follicular Thyroid Carcinoma, and Choriocarcinoma).
   * Veins are penetrated much more readily than arteries due to thinner walls.
   * *Primary Filter Organs:* **Liver** (receives all venous drainage from the portal GI tract) and **Lungs** (receives systemic venous drainage from the caval system) are the most frequent sites of hematogenous metastasis.
3. **Seeding of Body Cavities and Surfaces (Transcoelomic Spread):**
   * Occurs when a malignant neoplasm penetrates into a natural open body cavity (peritoneal, pleural, pericardial, subarachnoid spaces).
   * *Examples:* Ovarian carcinoma spreading across the peritoneal cavity; gastric signet-ring adenocarcinoma seeding both ovaries (**Krukenberg Tumor**).

## 5. Hematology & Lymphoid Pathology

### Iron Deficiency Anemia (IDA)
* **Definition & Etiology:** Microcytic, hypochromic anemia resulting from inadequate iron stores for hemoglobin synthesis.
  * Causes: Chronic blood loss (**most common in adults: GI bleeding from ulcers/hookworm, menorrhagia**), increased physiological requirements (pregnancy, lactation, childhood growth), poor dietary intake, malabsorption (Celiac disease, post-gastrectomy).
* **Laboratory Diagnosis:**
  1. *Complete Blood Count (CBC):*
     * Hemoglobin (Hb) and Hematocrit (PCV): Markedly reduced.
     * Red Cell Indices: **MCV < 80 fL** (Microcytic), **MCH < 27 pg** (Hypochromic), **MCHC < 32 g/dL**.
     * **Red Cell Distribution Width (RDW): Elevated (> 15%)**, indicating prominent anisopoikilocytosis (diagnostic distinction from $\beta$-thalassemia trait, where RDW is normal).
  2. *Peripheral Blood Smear:*
     * RBCs show marked **microcytosis and hypochromia** (central pallor exceeds 1/3 of the red cell diameter; cells may appear as thin rims of hemoglobin called **"pencil cells" / cigar cells** or target cells).
  3. *Serum Iron Studies:*
     * **Serum Ferritin: Markedly reduced (< 15 $\mu$g/L)**. *Single most specific and sensitive biochemical marker of depleted iron stores.*
     * Serum Iron: Decreased.
     * **Total Iron Binding Capacity (TIBC): Elevated** (> 400 $\mu$g/dL; liver increases transferrin synthesis).
     * Transferrin Saturation: Severely reduced (< 15%).
  4. *Bone Marrow Examination (Definitive Standard):*
     * Erythroid hyperplasia with micronormoblastic maturation.
     * **Perls' Prussian Blue Stain:** Shows a **complete absence of stainable hemosiderin iron** in bone marrow macrophages.

### Acute Myeloid Leukemia (AML)
* Clonal neoplastic proliferation of immature myeloid precursors (myeloblasts) accumulating in bone marrow, suppressing normal hematopoiesis. Defined by **$\ge 20\%$ myeloblasts** in bone marrow or peripheral blood (WHO criteria).
* **Laboratory Diagnosis:**
  * *CBC & Smear:* Severe normocytic normochromic anemia, profound thrombocytopenia, and variable total WBC count (leukocytosis or leukopenia) with circulating myeloblasts.
  * *Morphology of Myeloblast:* Large cell (15–20 $\mu$m), high N:C ratio, delicate fine lacy chromatin, 2–5 prominent nucleoli, moderate basophilic cytoplasm.
  * **Auer Rods (Pathognomonic):** Pink/red-staining, needle-like or spindle-shaped cytoplasmic crystalline inclusions formed by abnormal crystallization of fused azurophilic granules. Finding Auer rods definitively confirms a **myeloid lineage** (rules out ALL).
  * *Cytochemical Staining:* **Myeloperoxidase (MPO) positive** (strong granular brown-black cytoplasmic staining); **Sudan Black B (SBB) positive**; Periodic Acid-Schiff (PAS) negative or diffusely faint.

### Hodgkin Lymphoma & Reed-Sternberg (RS) Cell
* Malignant lymphoid neoplasm originating in B-cells of germinal centers, typically arising within a single lymph node chain and spreading predictably to contiguous lymph node groups.
* **Diagnostic Hallmark — The Reed-Sternberg (RS) Cell:**
  * The essential malignant neoplastic giant cell required for histopathological diagnosis of Hodgkin Lymphoma (though it constitutes only 1–5% of the total tumor mass, resting in an abundant background of non-neoplastic reactive inflammatory cells: small lymphocytes, histiocytes, eosinophils, and plasma cells).
  * *Classic RS Cell Morphology:*
    * Very large cell (15–45 $\mu$m in diameter).
    * **Binucleated or bilobed nucleus** with mirror-image symmetry (**"Owl-Eye" appearance**).
    * Each nuclear lobe contains a **gigantic, inclusion-like, eosinophilic nucleolus** surrounded by a distinct clear halo of nucleoplasm.
    * Abundant amphophilic or slightly basophilic cytoplasm.
  * *Immunophenotype of Classic RS Cells:* Typically **CD15 positive**, **CD30 positive**, and CD45 (LCA) negative.
