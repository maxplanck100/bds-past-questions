## March 7, 2022 (New Course, 50 Marks)

### Section B

**1.** Cell membrane transport classification: **Passive transport** (no energy, down concentration gradient) — simple diffusion (O₂, CO₂, lipid-soluble substances), facilitated diffusion (glucose, amino acids via carriers/channels), osmosis. **Active transport** — Primary (uses ATP directly, e.g., Na⁺-K⁺-ATPase) and Secondary (cotransport using gradient created by primary active transport, e.g., Na⁺-glucose in intestine).

**Why Vmax is reached in facilitated diffusion:** Carrier proteins (transporters) are saturable — when all carrier molecules are occupied by substrate, the transport rate reaches a maximum (Vmax) and cannot increase further despite rising substrate concentration, unlike simple diffusion which continues to rise linearly. This is analogous to enzyme kinetics (Km = concentration at half Vmax).

**Primary active transport:** Uses energy from ATP hydrolysis directly to move ions against their electrochemical gradients. Example: **Na⁺-K⁺-ATPase** — transports 3 Na⁺ out and 2 K⁺ in per ATP, maintaining the resting membrane potential, cell volume, and electrochemical gradients used by secondary active transport.

**2.** **Hemostasis** is the physiological process that stops bleeding from damaged vessels without causing excessive clotting. Natural anticoagulants preventing inappropriate clot formation:
- **Antithrombin III** — serine protease inhibitor; inactivates thrombin, Xa, IXa, XIa; enhanced 1000× by heparin.
- **Protein C & Protein S** — Vitamin K-dependent; activated protein C (with protein S as cofactor) inactivates factors Va and VIIIa.
- **Tissue Factor Pathway Inhibitor (TFPI)** — inhibits TF-VIIa complex.
- **Prostacyclin (PGI₂)** — from endothelium; inhibits platelet aggregation and causes vasodilation.
- **Thrombomodulin** — on endothelium; binds thrombin, redirecting it to activate protein C.

**3.** **Action potential** is a rapid, transient, all-or-none reversal of membrane potential triggered when threshold is reached (~−55 mV).

**Properties:**
1. **All-or-none law:** Full action potential fires or none at all.
2. **Threshold:** Must depolarise to −55 mV to trigger.
3. **Refractory period:** Absolute (during depolarisation and early repolarisation — no new AP possible); Relative (partial repolarisation — only a stronger-than-normal stimulus fires).
4. **Non-decremental conduction:** Amplitude constant along the axon.
5. **Unidirectional conduction:** Travels orthodromically due to refractory period of just-excited segment.

**4. Short notes:**

**4a. Stages of erythropoiesis**
In the red bone marrow: Pluripotent stem cell → BFU-E (burst-forming unit) → CFU-E → **Proerythroblast** (earliest recognisable RBC precursor) → **Basophilic erythroblast** (abundant ribosomes, haemoglobin synthesis begins) → **Polychromatophilic erythroblast** (both basophilic and pink on staining) → **Orthochromatophilic (normoblastic) erythroblast** (nucleus condenses) → **Nucleus extruded** → **Reticulocyte** (still contains ribosomal RNA; released into blood, 1–2% of circulating RBCs) → **Mature erythrocyte** (biconcave, anucleate). Takes ~7 days. Regulated by **erythropoietin (EPO)** from kidneys (hypoxia stimulus).

**4b. Landsteiner's law**
States that: (i) If an antigen is present on RBCs, the corresponding antibody is **absent** from the plasma. (ii) If an antigen is absent from RBCs, the corresponding antibody is **present** in the plasma. This law applies to the ABO blood group system. It explains naturally occurring ABO antibodies (IgM anti-A, anti-B) that develop without prior exposure to foreign red cells, and underpins pre-transfusion compatibility testing.

**4c. Effect of parasympathetic and sympathetic stimulation:**
| Organ | Parasympathetic | Sympathetic |
|-------|----------------|-------------|
| Pupil | Miosis (constricts — via circular muscle, CN III) | Mydriasis (dilates — via radial/dilator muscle, α₁) |
| Heart | Bradycardia (↓ HR, ↓ AV conduction — via M₂ receptors/vagus) | Tachycardia, ↑ contractility (β₁ receptors) |
| Bronchus | Bronchoconstriction, ↑ secretion (M₃) | Bronchodilation (β₂ receptors) |

### Section C

**5.** **Cardiac cycle** is the sequence of electrical and mechanical events in one complete heartbeat. Normal duration: **~0.8 s** at 75 bpm.

**Components:**
| Phase | Duration (s) | Description |
|-------|-------------|-------------|
| Atrial systole | 0.1 | Atria contract, complete ventricular filling |
| Isovolumetric contraction | 0.05 | AV valves close, semilunar valves not yet open, pressure rises |
| Rapid ejection | 0.11 | Semilunar valves open, rapid ejection of ~70% stroke volume |
| Reduced ejection | 0.13 | Slowing ejection, semilunar valves begin to close |
| Isovolumetric relaxation | 0.08 | All valves closed, pressure falls without change in volume |
| Rapid filling | 0.11 | AV valves open, passive rapid ventricular filling |
| Slow filling (diastasis) | 0.19 | Passive slow filling |

**Physiological significance:** Efficient one-way flow of blood, generation of heart sounds (S1 at AV closure, S2 at semilunar closure), coronary filling occurs during diastole (isovolumetric relaxation and diastasis).

**6.** **Mechanism of quiet inspiration:**
- Respiratory centre (pre-Bötzinger complex in medulla) fires rhythmically.
- Motor output via phrenic nerve (C3,4,5) → **diaphragm** contracts, descends 1.5 cm → thoracic volume increases.
- **External intercostal muscles** contract → ribs elevated and everted (pump-handle and bucket-handle movements) → AP and transverse diameter increase.
- Intrathoracic (intrapleural) pressure falls from −5 to −8 mmHg.
- Lung expands, intrapulmonary pressure falls below atmospheric (−1 mmHg) → air flows in.
- Lung fills to end-inspiratory volume.

**Quiet expiration:** Passive process.
- Inspiratory muscles relax.
- Elastic recoil of lung tissue and chest wall restores resting position.
- Intrapulmonary pressure rises above atmospheric (+1 mmHg) → air flows out.
- No active muscular effort needed for quiet expiration.

**7.** **Surfactant composition:** Dipalmitoylphosphatidylcholine (DPPC/lecithin) ~70%, other phospholipids ~10%, cholesterol ~10%, surfactant-associated proteins (SP-A, SP-B, SP-C, SP-D) ~10%.

**Effects of lack of surfactant:** Without surfactant, alveolar surface tension is high → alveoli tend to collapse (atelectasis) at end-expiration. Consequences:
- Increased work of breathing (breathing against high surface tension).
- Alveolar collapse → ventilation-perfusion mismatch → hypoxia.
- Protein-rich fluid leaks into alveoli → formation of **hyaline membranes** (pink, glassy appearance on histology).
- **Respiratory Distress Syndrome (RDS) / Hyaline Membrane Disease** in premature neonates; also seen in adults (ARDS).

**8. Short notes:**

**8a. Acclimatization to high altitude**

At high altitude, PO₂ is reduced (↓ barometric pressure). Immediate responses: hyperventilation (hypoxic drive), tachycardia, increased cardiac output. Acclimatization over days to weeks:
- **Respiratory:** Sustained hyperventilation → ↑ 2,3-BPG in RBCs (rightward shift of ODC — increased O₂ unloading to tissues).
- **Haematological:** Hypoxia → ↑ EPO (from kidney) → polycythaemia (increased RBC mass, haemoglobin concentration). Increased 2,3-BPG.
- **Cardiovascular:** Heart rate normalises; increased pulmonary vascularity.
- **Cellular:** Increased mitochondrial density, increased myoglobin.
- **Cerebral:** Normalisation of CSF pH as bicarbonate is excreted by kidneys (renalcompensation for respiratory alkalosis).

**8b. Functions of lymphatic system**
1. **Fluid balance:** Returns ~2–4 L/day of interstitial fluid (ultrafiltrate excess) to circulation, preventing oedema.
2. **Lipid absorption:** Chylomicrons (dietary fats) absorbed into lacteals in intestinal villi → thoracic duct → blood.
3. **Immune function:** Lymph nodes filter lymph; lymphocytes and macrophages in nodes destroy pathogens and foreign particles.
4. **Protein return:** Returns plasma proteins that have leaked into interstitial fluid.
5. **Removal of cellular debris and waste products.**

**8c. Cardiac output**

**Cardiac output (CO)** = Heart Rate (HR) × Stroke Volume (SV). Normal: ~5 L/min (70 bpm × 70 mL). **Stroke volume** determined by preload (EDV), afterload (arterial resistance), and contractility. **Factors increasing CO:** Exercise (SV + HR increase), pregnancy, fever, AV fistula, hyperthyroidism. **Measured by:** Fick principle (CO = O₂ consumption / A-V O₂ difference), thermodilution (Swan-Ganz catheter), echocardiography (Doppler).

### Section B

**1.** Functions of membrane proteins: (i) Transport (channels, carriers, pumps). (ii) Receptors for hormones, neurotransmitters. (iii) Enzymes (e.g., Na⁺-K⁺-ATPase). (iv) Structural anchoring (connecting cytoskeleton to extracellular matrix). (v) Cell recognition/adhesion (glycoproteins). (vi) Transduction (linking extracellular signal to intracellular response).

**Simple vs. facilitated diffusion:**
| Feature | Simple Diffusion | Facilitated Diffusion |
|---------|----------------|----------------------|
| Carrier | No carrier | Uses specific carrier proteins or channels |
| Saturation | No Vmax | Vmax reached (saturable) |
| Specificity | Non-specific | Specific for substrate |
| Inhibition | Not inhibitable | Inhibited by competitors |
| Examples | O₂, CO₂, N₂, ethanol | Glucose (GLUT), amino acids |

**Secondary active transport example:** Na⁺-glucose cotransport (SGLT1) in intestinal brush border — Na⁺ gradient (maintained by Na⁺-K⁺-ATPase) drives glucose uphill into enterocytes; Na⁺ and glucose move together.

**2.** Steps in hemostasis: (i) Vascular spasm (vasoconstriction). (ii) Primary platelet plug formation. (iii) Secondary hemostasis (coagulation cascade) → fibrin clot. (iv) Fibrinolysis (clot resolution).

**Platelet role in clot retraction:** After fibrin network forms, platelets contract via actomyosin (using ATP), pulling on fibrin strands and compacting the clot → serum is expressed → clot becomes firm and seals the vessel wall. Powered by platelet contractile proteins.

**Extrinsic pathway (flow chart):**
Tissue injury → TF (tissue factor) released → TF + VIIa → TF-VIIa complex → activates X → Xa + Va (prothrombinase complex) + phospholipid + Ca²⁺ → Prothrombin → **Thrombin** → Fibrinogen → **Fibrin** (XIIIa cross-links fibrin).

**3.** **Ionic basis of action potential in nerve:**
- **Resting:** K⁺ conductance high; membrane at −70 mV (Nernst equilibrium near K⁺ equilibrium potential). Na⁺ channels closed.
- **Depolarisation:** Stimulus reaches threshold (−55 mV) → **voltage-gated Na⁺ channels open** → Na⁺ rushes in → membrane rapidly depolarises to +30 mV (Na⁺ equilibrium potential).
- **Repolarisation:** Na⁺ channels inactivate (inactivation gate closes, h-gate); **voltage-gated K⁺ channels open** (delayed rectifier) → K⁺ rushes out → membrane repolarises.
- **Hyperpolarisation (after-potential):** K⁺ channels remain open briefly → membrane overshoots below resting potential.
- **Restoration:** Na⁺-K⁺-ATPase restores ionic concentrations.

*(Draw: action potential graph with phases: depolarisation peak, repolarisation, hyperpolarisation, resting.)*

**4. Short notes:**

**4a. Hemostasis**
(See March 7, 2022 Q2 above.)

**4b. Three important roles of Na⁺-K⁺ pump (Na⁺-K⁺-ATPase)**
1. **Maintains resting membrane potential:** By creating a K⁺-rich interior and Na⁺-rich exterior, establishes the ionic gradients that make the cell excitable.
2. **Regulates cell volume:** Na⁺ export prevents osmotic swelling; if the pump is blocked, cells swell and lyse.
3. **Provides driving force for secondary active transport:** Na⁺ gradient (low intracellular Na⁺) powers cotransporters (SGLT, amino acid transporters) and antiporters (Na⁺-H⁺ exchanger).

**4c. Cyclic AMP second messenger pathway**
Hormone (e.g., adrenaline, glucagon) binds → **Gs protein-coupled receptor (GPCR)** → Gαs activated → **adenylyl cyclase** activated → ATP → **cAMP** → activates **protein kinase A (PKA)** → PKA phosphorylates target proteins → physiological response (e.g., glycogen breakdown in liver, heart rate increase, lipolysis). Terminated by: phosphodiesterase (cAMP → 5'-AMP); G-protein GTPase activity. Opposite: Gi protein-coupled receptors inhibit adenylyl cyclase.

### Section C

**5.** Four forces influencing fluid movement across capillary wall (**Starling forces**):
1. **Capillary hydrostatic pressure (Pc):** ~35 mmHg arterial end; pushes fluid out of capillary.
2. **Interstitial fluid hydrostatic pressure (Pi):** ~−2 mmHg; slightly negative (subatmospheric), facilitates fluid movement out of capillary.
3. **Plasma colloid osmotic pressure (πc):** ~25 mmHg; due to plasma proteins (especially albumin); draws fluid back into capillary.
4. **Interstitial colloid osmotic pressure (πi):** ~5 mmHg; due to proteins in interstitium; draws fluid out of capillary.

**Net filtration pressure = (Pc + πi) − (Pi + πc).** At arterial end: ~13 mmHg favouring filtration. At venous end: ~7 mmHg favouring reabsorption. Excess fluid is returned by lymphatics. Oedema occurs when net filtration exceeds lymphatic return.

**6.** **Lung volumes:**
| Volume/Capacity | Normal Value | Definition |
|----------------|-------------|------------|
| Tidal volume (TV) | 500 mL | Inspired/expired in quiet breathing |
| Inspiratory reserve volume (IRV) | 3000 mL | Extra air inspired above TV |
| Expiratory reserve volume (ERV) | 1100 mL | Extra air expired after normal expiration |
| Residual volume (RV) | 1200 mL | Air remaining after maximal expiration |
| Inspiratory capacity (IC) | TV + IRV = 3500 mL | |
| Functional residual capacity (FRC) | ERV + RV = 2300 mL | Air remaining after quiet expiration |
| Vital capacity (VC) | TV + IRV + ERV = 4600 mL | Max air expired after max inspiration |
| Total lung capacity (TLC) | VC + RV = 5800 mL | Total air in lungs after max inspiration |

RV and FRC cannot be measured by spirometry alone (require helium dilution or body plethysmography).

**7.** **Cardiac output:** (See March 7, 2022 Q8c above for definition.)

**Factors affecting cardiac output:**
- **Heart rate:** Sympathetic (↑HR), parasympathetic (↓HR), temperature, hormones.
- **Stroke volume — preload:** EDV/venous return (Frank-Starling); increased by IV fluids, exercise (muscle pump), posture (lying).
- **Stroke volume — afterload:** Aortic pressure/peripheral resistance; increased afterload (hypertension) ↓SV.
- **Stroke volume — contractility (inotropy):** Sympathetic stimulation, catecholamines, digoxin ↑ contractility; hypoxia, acidosis, myocardial disease ↓ contractility.

**8. Short notes:**

**8a. Chemical regulation of respiration**
Peripheral chemoreceptors (carotid and aortic bodies): primarily respond to ↓PO₂ (<60 mmHg, rapidly), also to ↑PCO₂ and ↓pH. Signal via CN IX and X. Central chemoreceptors (ventrolateral medulla): primarily respond to ↑PCO₂/↑H⁺ in CSF (CO₂ crosses blood-brain barrier; forms H⁺ from CO₂ + H₂O → H₂CO₃ → H⁺ + HCO₃⁻). O₂ does not significantly stimulate central chemoreceptors. Together they maintain PaCO₂ ~40 mmHg and PaO₂ >60 mmHg.

**8b. Local metabolic changes influencing arteriolar radius**
Active metabolic vasodilation: ↑CO₂, ↓O₂, ↑H⁺, ↑K⁺, ↑adenosine, ↑osmolality, ↑temperature, ↑lactic acid → local vasodilation (arteriolar dilation) → ↑blood flow to active tissue. Autoregulation of blood flow. Nitric oxide (NO) from endothelium: potent vasodilator (activates guanylyl cyclase → cGMP → smooth muscle relaxation). Myogenic response: arterioles constrict in response to stretch (rising transmural pressure) — maintains constant flow over a range of pressures.

**8c. Types of hypoxia**
1. **Hypoxic (arterial) hypoxia:** Low PaO₂. Causes: High altitude, hypoventilation, V/Q mismatch (pneumonia, pulmonary oedema), diffusion defect (pulmonary fibrosis), right-to-left shunt.
2. **Anaemic hypoxia:** Normal PaO₂ but reduced O₂ carrying capacity. Causes: anaemia, methaemoglobinaemia, CO poisoning (COHb).
3. **Circulatory (stagnant) hypoxia:** Normal PaO₂ and Hb but reduced blood flow. Causes: cardiac failure, shock, local ischaemia.
4. **Histotoxic hypoxia:** Normal O₂ delivery but cells cannot use O₂. Cause: cyanide poisoning (inhibits cytochrome c oxidase).

**1.** Passive transport: movement down an electrochemical gradient without energy. Examples: Simple diffusion (O₂, CO₂), facilitated diffusion (glucose via GLUT1). Active transport: movement against electrochemical gradient requiring energy (ATP or gradient). Examples: Na⁺-K⁺-ATPase (primary), Na⁺-glucose cotransport (secondary).

**2.** Stages of erythropoiesis: (See March 7, 2022 Q4a above for complete stages.)

*(Draw: sequential cell diagram from proerythroblast to mature RBC, showing decreasing size, chromatin condensation, Hb appearance, nucleus loss.)*

**3.** Ionic basis of action potential: (See Nov 2, 2021 Q3 above.)

**Graded potential vs. action potential:**
| Feature | Graded Potential | Action Potential |
|---------|----------------|-----------------|
| Amplitude | Variable | All-or-none (~100 mV) |
| Conduction | Decremental (diminishes with distance) | Non-decremental |
| Summation | Can summate | Cannot |
| Site | Dendrites/soma, sensory receptors | Axon hillock, axon |
| Cause | Ligand-gated channels | Voltage-gated channels |

**4. Short notes:**

**4a. Gap junction**
Specialised intercellular connections made of **connexon** proteins (hexamers of connexins). Two connexons (one from each cell) align to form a hydrophilic channel of ~1.5 nm diameter. Allow direct passage of small molecules and ions (< 1000 Da) between adjacent cells. Functions: Electrical coupling (cardiac muscle → synchronised contraction as functional syncytium); metabolic coupling; signal propagation in smooth muscle. Absent in skeletal muscle.

**4b. Effect of sympathetic stimulation on the heart**
Via β₁-adrenoreceptors: (i) **Chronotropy** (↑ HR) — increases slope of pacemaker potential. (ii) **Inotropy** (↑ contractility) — ↑ intracellular cAMP → PKA phosphorylates L-type Ca²⁺ channels → more Ca²⁺ entry → stronger contraction. (iii) **Dromotropy** (↑ AV conduction velocity). (iv) **Lusitropy** (↑ relaxation speed). Overall: larger stroke volume, faster heart rate → ↑ cardiac output for fight-or-flight response.

**4c. Role of T-helper cells in immune activation**
CD4⁺ T-helper cells (Th) are the "master regulators" of adaptive immunity. When a dendritic cell presents antigen on MHC II → Th cell recognises via TCR + CD4 → Th cell activates and differentiates: **Th1** (promotes cell-mediated immunity — activates macrophages via IFN-γ; drives CD8⁺ cytotoxic T cells); **Th2** (promotes humoral immunity — IL-4,5,13 → B-cell activation, IgE, eosinophil response); **Th17** (mucosal immunity, neutrophil recruitment — IL-17). Th cells also provide "help" to B cells for antibody class-switching and affinity maturation.

### Section C

**5.** Short-term, intermediate, and long-term mechanisms of blood pressure regulation:

**Short-term (seconds to minutes):** Neural reflexes:
- **Baroreceptor reflex** (carotid sinus, aortic arch) — most important; stretch → CN IX/X → medullary cardiovascular centre → ↓ sympathetic + ↑ parasympathetic → ↓ HR, ↓ TPR, ↓ BP. Acts within seconds.
- **Chemoreceptor reflex** (carotid/aortic bodies, hypoxia) → ↑ sympathetic → ↑ BP.
- **CNS ischaemic response** (severe hypoxia of brain stem) → massive sympathetic discharge.

**Intermediate (minutes to hours):**
- Capillary fluid shift (Starling forces redistribute fluid between compartments).
- Renin-Angiotensin-Aldosterone System (RAAS) — begins within minutes but takes hours for full effect.
- Stress relaxation (vessel wall).

**Long-term (days to weeks):**
- **Renal pressure-natriuresis mechanism** — most powerful; ↑BP → ↑renal Na⁺ and water excretion → ↓blood volume → ↓BP (infinite gain).
- **RAAS** full effect (aldosterone causes Na⁺ retention).
- **ADH (vasopressin)** — causes water retention + vasoconstriction.
- Cardiovascular remodelling (hypertension → LVH).

**6.** **Haldane effect:** CO₂ transport is influenced by the oxygenation state of haemoglobin. Deoxygenated haemoglobin (deoxy-Hb) binds CO₂ as carbamino compounds more readily and accepts H⁺ ions better (Hb is a better buffer when deoxygenated) → **tissues**: O₂ unloaded → deoxy-Hb formed → more CO₂ carried. **Lungs**: O₂ loaded → oxy-Hb formed → CO₂ released. The Haldane effect accounts for ~60% of CO₂ transport enhancement. **Significance:** Efficient CO₂ removal from tissues and excretion in lungs; contributes to the venous-arterial difference in CO₂ content (~4 mL/100 mL).

*(Draw: CO₂ dissociation curve — oxygenated Hb curve lies to the right and below deoxygenated Hb curve, demonstrating that oxygenated blood carries less CO₂ at any given PCO₂.)*

**7.** **Hypoxia:** Deficiency of O₂ at the tissue level. Types: (see Nov 2, 2021 Q8c above).

**Hypoxic hypoxia in detail:** PaO₂ falls below 60 mmHg → peripheral chemoreceptors (carotid bodies, CN IX) stimulated → hyperventilation → ↓PaCO₂ → respiratory alkalosis. Peripheral vasodilation, cerebral vasodilation. At altitude: chronically ↑ ventilation, polycythaemia, ↑ 2,3-BPG. Effects: headache, confusion, ataxia (cerebral hypoxia); cyanosis (if >5 g/dL of deoxy-Hb). Caused by: altitude, hypoventilation (CNS depression, neuromuscular disease), V/Q mismatch (COPD, pneumonia), diffusion defect, shunt.

**8. Short notes:**

**8a. Conducting system of heart**
SA node (pacemaker, 60–100 bpm, right atrium at SVC junction) → **Atrial conduction** via internodal pathways and Bachmann's bundle (to left atrium) → **AV node** (delay ~0.1 s; only electrical connection between atria and ventricles; intrinsic rate 40–60 bpm) → **Bundle of His** (penetrates fibrous skeleton) → **Left and right bundle branches** → **Purkinje fibres** (spread rapidly throughout ventricular myocardium, intrinsic rate 20–40 bpm) → ventricular myocardium. The AV delay allows atria to complete contraction before ventricular systole.

**8b. Chemical regulation of respiration**
(See Nov 2, 2021 Q8a above.)

**8c. Vital capacity**
Vital capacity (VC) is the maximum volume of air that can be expelled from the lungs after a maximum inspiration. VC = IRV + TV + ERV = ~4600 mL in a healthy adult male. Affected by: posture (↓ supine), age (↓), sex (↑ male), height (↑ taller), lung compliance. **Clinical use:** Timed vital capacity (FEV₁ and FVC ratio) — FEV₁/FVC <70% indicates **obstructive** disease (asthma, COPD); FEV₁/FVC ≥70% but reduced FVC indicates **restrictive** disease (fibrosis, effusion).

### Section B

**1.** Erythropoiesis (see March 26, 2021 Q2 above). Essential factors and roles:
- **Iron:** Component of haem; deficiency causes microcytic hypochromic anaemia.
- **Vitamin B12 (cobalamin):** Required for DNA synthesis (thymidylate synthesis via folate cycle); deficiency → megaloblastic anaemia.
- **Folic acid:** Required for purine and thymidylate synthesis; deficiency → megaloblastic anaemia.
- **Erythropoietin (EPO):** Glycoprotein hormone (kidneys); drives proliferation and differentiation of CFU-E; released by peritubular fibroblasts in response to hypoxia.
- **Vitamin B6 (pyridoxine):** Required for haem synthesis (ALA synthase cofactor).
- **Vitamin C:** Required for iron absorption.
- **Copper:** Required for haemoglobin synthesis and iron mobilisation.

**2.** Types of membrane transport: (see March 7, 2022 Q1 above for full classification.) Additional: **Endocytosis** (pinocytosis, phagocytosis, receptor-mediated endocytosis) and **Exocytosis** (secretory vesicle fusion with plasma membrane). **Osmosis** (water movement across semipermeable membrane down its concentration gradient).

**3.** Cells in blood: RBCs (erythrocytes), WBCs (leukocytes), Platelets (thrombocytes).

**White blood cells and functions:**
| WBC | Normal % | Functions |
|-----|----------|-----------|
| Neutrophil | 60–70% | Phagocytosis (first responder, bacterial infections) |
| Lymphocyte | 20–30% | B cells (antibody), T cells (cellular immunity), NK cells |
| Monocyte | 3–8% | Phagocytosis, differentiate into macrophages |
| Eosinophil | 1–4% | Anti-parasitic, allergic responses |
| Basophil | 0–1% | Release histamine/heparin; allergy, inflammation |

**4. Short notes:**

**4a. Negative feedback with an example**
Negative feedback maintains homeostasis by a response that opposes the initial change, returning the variable to its set point. Example: **Thermoregulation** — rise in core body temperature → detected by anterior hypothalamus → responses: sweating (evaporation), cutaneous vasodilation (heat loss) → temperature falls back to 37°C. Another example: **Baroreceptor reflex** — ↑BP → baroreceptors fire → ↓HR, ↓TPR → BP normalised. Without negative feedback, systems are unstable.

**4b. Macrophage**
Mononuclear phagocytic cells derived from blood monocytes (which originate from bone marrow precursors). Present in most tissues (Kupffer cells in liver, microglia in brain, alveolar macrophages in lung, osteoclasts in bone). Functions: (i) Phagocytosis of pathogens, dead cells, debris. (ii) Antigen presentation (MHC II) to T cells. (iii) Cytokine production (IL-1, IL-6, TNF-α) — mediate inflammation. (iv) Role in adaptive immunity (activate Th cells). (v) Wound healing (secrete growth factors). Activated by: IFN-γ (from Th1), LPS.

**4c. Presynaptic ganglia in autonomic fibres**
Autonomic preganglionic neurons have their cell bodies in the CNS (lateral horn). The **preganglionic fibre** (myelinated, B fibre) travels to an autonomic ganglion and synapses on the postganglionic neuron. Ganglia are located differently in sympathetic (paravertebral chain or prevertebral ganglia — far from target organs, so short preganglionic/long postganglionic fibre) vs. parasympathetic (terminal ganglia near or within target organs — long preganglionic/short postganglionic fibre). Neurotransmitter at all preganglionic synapses: **acetylcholine (ACh)** acting on nicotinic receptors.

**5.** Cardiac cycle duration at 75 bpm: **0.8 seconds** (systole ~0.3s, diastole ~0.5s). Phases: (See March 7, 2022 Q5 above for full table with durations.)

*(Draw: Wiggers diagram — LV pressure curve, aortic pressure, LV volume, ECG, and heart sounds (S1, S2) aligned against time.)*

**6.** Vital capacity defined and measured — (see March 26, 2021 Q8c above). **Timed vital capacity (FEV₁):** Volume expelled in the first second of a maximal forced expiration after a full inspiration. Normal: FEV₁/FVC ≥ 80%. In obstructive disease: FEV₁ ↓↓, FVC normal or ↓ → ratio ↓. In restrictive disease: FEV₁ ↓ but FVC ↓↓ proportionally → ratio normal. Relevance: Distinguish obstructive from restrictive patterns; monitor asthma, COPD, fibrosis.

**7.** **Baroreceptor reflex** (short-term BP regulation):
Baroreceptors = mechanoreceptors in the **carotid sinus** (CN IX, Hering's nerve) and **aortic arch** (CN X, depressor nerve). They sense wall stretch (proxy for pressure). ↑ BP → ↑ firing rate → signals to NTS (nucleus tractus solitarius) in medulla → ↑ parasympathetic (vagal) outflow to heart (↓ HR) + ↓ sympathetic outflow (↓ TPR, ↓ cardiac contractility) → BP falls. ↓ BP → reverse: ↓ baroreceptor firing → ↑ sympathetic → ↑ HR, ↑ contractility, vasoconstriction → BP rises. This operates within **seconds** and is reset in chronic hypertension.

**8. Short notes:**

**8a. Acclimatization** (See March 7, 2022 Q8a above.)

**8b. Heart sounds**
- **S1 ("lubb"):** Closure of **mitral** (M1) and **tricuspid** (T1) valves at the start of ventricular systole (beginning of isovolumetric contraction). Best heard at the apex.
- **S2 ("dubb"):** Closure of **aortic** (A2) and **pulmonary** (P2) valves at the end of ventricular systole (beginning of diastole). Normally split on inspiration (delayed P2). Heard at the base.
- **S3 (gallop):** Rapid ventricular filling in early diastole; pathological in adults (indicates HF, MR); normal in children.
- **S4:** Atrial contraction against a non-compliant (stiff) ventricle; indicates ventricular hypertrophy.

**8c. Dead space**
Volume of inspired air not participating in gas exchange.
- **Anatomical dead space:** Volume of conducting airways (trachea to terminal bronchioles) where no gas exchange occurs (~150 mL or ~2 mL/kg).
- **Alveolar dead space:** Alveoli ventilated but not perfused (e.g., pulmonary embolism).
- **Physiological dead space = anatomical + alveolar** (normally ≈ anatomical in health; increased in disease). **Bohr equation:** VD/VT = (PaCO₂ − PeCO₂)/PaCO₂. Dead space dilutes tidal volume → wasted ventilation.

### Section B

**1.** **Extrinsic pathway:** Activated by tissue factor (TF); TF + VIIa → X activation; fast (seconds). **Intrinsic pathway:** Contact activation (Hageman factor XII → XI → IX → VIIIa → X); slower (minutes). Both converge at Factor X activation (common pathway) → Xa + Va (prothrombinase) → Prothrombin → Thrombin → Fibrinogen → Fibrin (cross-linked by XIIIa). The **PT (prothrombin time)** tests the extrinsic pathway; **aPTT (activated partial thromboplastin time)** tests the intrinsic pathway.

**2.** **Cotransport:** Secondary active transport using two solutes moving in the same direction, driven by the electrochemical gradient of one (usually Na⁺). Examples: (i) **SGLT1** (Na⁺-glucose cotransport, small intestine and kidney proximal tubule). (ii) **Na⁺-amino acid cotransporters** in intestine. Mechanism: Na⁺ gradient (maintained by Na⁺-K⁺-ATPase) makes it energetically favourable for Na⁺ to enter; carrier protein uses this energy to simultaneously move the co-transported substance uphill. *(Draw: cartoon of SGLT1 with Na⁺ and glucose moving together into the cell.)*

**4. Short notes:**

**4a. Rh blood group**
Rh (Rhesus) system has >50 antigens; clinically most important is **D antigen.** Rh+ individuals have D antigen on RBCs (~85% of population); Rh− do not. Unlike ABO, Rh antibodies are not naturally occurring — they are formed only after exposure (transfusion, pregnancy). **Haemolytic disease of the newborn (HDN/erythroblastosis foetalis):** Rh− mother sensitised by Rh+ fetal cells → anti-D IgG → crosses placenta in subsequent pregnancy → destroys fetal RBCs → jaundice, hydrops fetalis. Prevention: **Anti-D immunoglobulin (Rh prophylaxis)** given to Rh− mothers within 72 hours of delivery/miscarriage/abortion.

**4b. Platelets**
Anucleate, discoid cells (2–4 µm) derived from megakaryocyte cytoplasm. Normal count: 150,000–400,000/µL. Life span: ~10 days. **Structure:** Dense granules (ADP, serotonin, Ca²⁺), α-granules (fibrinogen, vWF, P-selectin, PAF, clotting factors). **Functions:** (i) Adhesion (vWF bridge to collagen via GpIb). (ii) Activation (shape change, granule release). (iii) Aggregation (ADP, thromboxane A₂ recruit more platelets; GpIIb/IIIa-fibrinogen cross-links). (iv) Procoagulant surface (provide phosphatidylserine for tenase and prothrombinase complexes). (v) Clot retraction (see Nov 2, 2021 Q2 above).

**4c. Neutrophil**
Most numerous WBC (60–70%, 2,000–7,500/µL). Polymorphonuclear (multilobed nucleus). Life span 6–8 hours in blood, 1–4 days in tissue. **Functions:** First line of defence against bacteria. (i) **Chemotaxis:** Attracted by IL-8, C5a, LTB4, fMLP. (ii) **Phagocytosis:** Engulf opsonised (IgG/C3b-coated) bacteria into phagosomes. (iii) **Killing:** Respiratory burst (NADPH oxidase → O₂•⁻ → H₂O₂ → HOCl via myeloperoxidase), granule enzymes (elastase, protease). (iv) **NETs** (neutrophil extracellular traps): Chromatin + granule proteins trap and kill bacteria. Deficiency → recurrent bacterial infections.

### Section C

**5.** **Frank-Starling law of the heart:**
**Meaning (Starling's law):** The energy of contraction of cardiac muscle is proportional to the initial length of the cardiac muscle fibre, up to a physiological limit. In practical terms: The more the ventricle is filled during diastole (greater EDV/preload), the more forcefully it contracts and the greater the stroke volume.

**Mechanism:** ↑ EDV → ↑ cardiomyocyte stretch → optimal overlap of actin and myosin filaments → more cross-bridges → ↑ force of contraction → ↑ SV. Also: stretch → increased Ca²⁺ sensitivity of troponin C.

**Implication:** Allows the heart to automatically match SV to venous return; balances output of right and left ventricles; compensates for mild increases in afterload.

**8. Short notes:**

**8a. Hering-Breuer inflation reflex**
When the lungs are inflated beyond a critical volume, stretch receptors in the bronchial walls (pulmonary mechanoreceptors, slowly adapting) send impulses via the vagus nerve (CN X) to the **apneustic and pneumotaxic centres**, inhibiting the inspiratory drive → inspiration terminates. This prevents over-inflation of the lungs. In adults, it operates mainly during deep breathing (>1 L tidal volume) and anaesthesia; less important for normal quiet breathing. Plays a role in determining **respiratory frequency and tidal volume**.

**8b. Capacitance vessels**
Veins and venules are capacitance vessels because they are thin-walled, compliant (distensible), and contain ~60–70% of total blood volume at any time. Small changes in venous tone (via sympathetic innervation of venous smooth muscle) can significantly alter venous return (preload). **Venous return** determines EDV → affects SV (Frank-Starling). During haemorrhage: venoconstriction mobilises this reservoir. Compared to arteries (resistance vessels), veins have much higher compliance (capacitance = dV/dP).

### Section C

**5.** Changes in volume and pressure in the left ventricle during cardiac cycle: (See March 7, 2022 Q5 and Nov 2, 2021 Q5 — combined the Wiggers diagram description.)

Key events: Mitral valve closes (S1) → isovolumetric contraction (constant LV volume, rising pressure) → aortic valve opens (when LV pressure > aortic pressure, ~80 mmHg) → ejection (LV volume drops from ~130 mL EDV to ~60 mL ESV → SV = 70 mL) → aortic valve closes (S2) → isovolumetric relaxation (constant volume, falling pressure) → mitral valve opens (when LV pressure < LA pressure) → rapid then slow filling.

**6.** **Oxy-haemoglobin dissociation curve:** S-shaped (sigmoidal) curve of % Hb saturation vs. PO₂. **Information obtained:** (i) P₅₀ = PO₂ at which Hb is 50% saturated (~26.5 mmHg normally). (ii) Steep part (20–60 mmHg): Small ↑ PO₂ → large ↑ saturation — arterial loading in lungs. (iii) Flat part (60–100 mmHg): PO₂ can drop considerably with little change in saturation — safety margin.

**Factors shifting curve:**
- **Right shift (↓ affinity, ↑ O₂ unloading):** ↑ PCO₂, ↑ H⁺, ↑ temperature, ↑ 2,3-BPG (Bohr effect + BPG effect). **Favours tissue O₂ delivery.**
- **Left shift (↑ affinity, ↓ O₂ unloading):** ↓ PCO₂, ↓ H⁺, ↓ temperature, ↓ 2,3-BPG, HbF, CO (carboxyhaemoglobin). **Favours O₂ loading in lungs but impairs delivery to tissues.**

**7.** Changes in intrapulmonary and intrapleural pressures during inspiration and expiration: *(Draw: two graphs vs. time — intrapulmonary pressure oscillates between −1 mmHg (inspiration, below atmospheric) and +1 mmHg (expiration); intrapleural pressure oscillates between −5 mmHg (end-expiration) and −8 mmHg (end-inspiration), always negative.)*

**8. Short notes:**

**8a. Frank-Starling law** (See Jan 7, 2019 Q5 above.)

**8b. Control of heart rate**
**Intrinsic:** SA node pacemaker — phase 4 depolarisation (If — funny current, slow inward Na⁺/K⁺; ICa,L — L-type Ca²⁺ channels). Intrinsic rate ~100 bpm.
**Extrinsic:** Parasympathetic (vagus, dominant at rest) → ACh → M₂ receptor → ↑K⁺ conductance → hyperpolarisation → slows phase 4 → ↓HR. Sympathetic → noradrenaline → β₁ → cAMP → ↑If + ICa,L → steeper phase 4 → ↑HR. Hormones: adrenaline, thyroid hormone ↑HR. Temperature: ↑1°C → ↑HR ~10 bpm.

**8c. Carbon dioxide transport in blood**
CO₂ transported in 3 forms: (i) **Dissolved in plasma** (~7%): physically dissolved (CO₂ solubility = 0.03 mL/100 mL/mmHg). (ii) **As bicarbonate** (HCO₃⁻, ~70%): CO₂ + H₂O → H₂CO₃ → H⁺ + HCO₃⁻ (catalysed by carbonic anhydrase in RBCs); HCO₃⁻ exchanged out for Cl⁻ (chloride shift); H⁺ buffered by Hb. (iii) **Carbamino compounds** (~23%): CO₂ binds to amino groups of Hb (especially deoxy-Hb — Haldane effect). Total venous-arterial difference: ~4 mL CO₂/100 mL blood.

### Section B

**1.** **Erythroblastosis foetalis:** Haemolytic disease of the newborn from maternal IgG anti-D (or other Rh/ABO) crossing placenta, destroying fetal RBCs → haemolytic anaemia, jaundice (kernicterus if severe), hepatosplenomegaly, hydrops fetalis.

**Mismatched blood transfusion consequences:** Intravascular haemolysis (complement activation → IgM-mediated lysis of incompatible RBCs) → fever, chills, haemoglobinuria, renal failure, DIC, cardiovascular collapse, death in severe cases. Due to: pre-formed ABO IgM antibodies.

**2.** Ionic basis of action potential: (see Nov 2, 2021 Q3 above.)

**EPSP vs. IPSP:**
- **Excitatory postsynaptic potential (EPSP):** Produced by opening of Na⁺/K⁺ channels (e.g., nicotinic ACh receptors, AMPA glutamate receptors) → net inward current → partial depolarisation toward threshold. EPSPs summate (spatially + temporally) to trigger an AP at the axon hillock.
- **Inhibitory postsynaptic potential (IPSP):** Produced by opening of Cl⁻ channels (GABA-A, glycine receptors) → inward Cl⁻ current (hyperpolarisation) or K⁺ channels → moves membrane away from threshold. IPSPs make it harder to fire.

**3.** Methods of membrane transport: (see March 7, 2022 Q1 above.) **Facilitated diffusion:** Carrier-mediated, down concentration gradient, saturable, specific, does not require ATP. Examples: GLUT transporters for glucose (GLUT1 in RBC, GLUT2 in liver/β-cell, GLUT4 in muscle/adipose — insulin-dependent), amino acid transporters.

## June 5, 2016 and October 21, 2016 — New Course (50 Marks)

Key questions covered in previous sections. Additional topics:

**Homeostasis:** The maintenance of a relatively stable internal environment (body temperature, pH, blood glucose, osmolality, blood pressure) despite external fluctuations. Achieved by: Receptors (detect change), Control centre (process information; typically hypothalamus, medullary centres), Effectors (produce corrective response). Regulatory mechanisms: Negative feedback (most common, stabilising); Positive feedback (e.g., parturition, blood clotting — amplifying).

**Erythropoietin (EPO):** Glycoprotein hormone (34 kDa) produced by peritubular fibroblasts in renal cortex (90%) and liver (10%) in response to hypoxia (HIF-1α transcription factor). Acts on BFU-E and CFU-E in bone marrow → promotes erythropoiesis. Used therapeutically in anaemia of chronic renal disease; misused in doping (increases RBC mass and O₂ carrying capacity). Normal serum EPO: 4–26 mIU/mL; rises 100–1000× in severe anaemia/hypoxia.

**Windkessel effect:** Elastic arteries (aorta, major branches) distend during systole storing energy → recoil during diastole maintaining diastolic pressure and continuous flow through the peripheral circulation. This converts the pulsatile output of the heart into a more continuous flow. In atherosclerosis: arteries stiffen → ↑ pulse pressure → ↑ systolic hypertension → more cardiac work.

**Coronary circulation:** (i) Coronary arteries fill during diastole (ventricular contraction compresses intramural vessels during systole). (ii) LCA more susceptible to compression during systole. (iii) Supply: RCA (right side, SA, AV nodes in right-dominant); LCA LAD (anterior IVS, LV anterior), LCx (lateral LV). (iv) Autoregulation: Local metabolic vasodilation. Angina pectoris: ischaemia during increased demand (exertion).

**SA node:** Pacemaker of the heart. Located in the right atrial wall near the SVC junction. Generates spontaneous action potentials ~60–100/min due to slow phase 4 depolarisation (If current and T-type Ca²⁺ current). Automaticity: decremental membrane potential from −60 mV (no stable resting potential). Slower pacemakers (AV node, Purkinje) are suppressed by the faster SA rate (overdrive suppression).
