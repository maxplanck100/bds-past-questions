### Section B [25 Marks]

#### 1. Enlist the type of synovial joint with one example of each. [6]
Synovial joints are freely movable joints characterised by a synovial cavity, articular cartilage, fibrous capsule, and synovial fluid. They are classified based on the shape of articulating surfaces and axes of movement:

1. **Plane Synovial Joint (Non-axial):** Flat articular surfaces permitting gliding/sliding movements.
   - *Example:* Acromioclavicular joint, intercarpal joints.
2. **Hinge Joint / Ginglymus (Uniaxial):** Spool-shaped surface fitting into a concave surface; movements in transverse axis (flexion/extension).
   - *Example:* Elbow joint (humero-ulnar), interphalangeal joints.
3. **Pivot Joint / Trochoid (Uniaxial):** Central bony pivot surrounded by an osteoligamentous ring; rotation around longitudinal axis.
   - *Example:* Atlanto-axial joint (median), superior and inferior radio-ulnar joints.
4. **Condylar / Bicondylar Joint (Modified Biaxial):** Two distinct convex condyles articulating with shallow concave surfaces; flexion, extension, limited rotation.
   - *Example:* Knee joint, temporomandibular joint (TMJ).
5. **Ellipsoid Joint (Biaxial):** Oval convex condyle received into an elliptic concavity; flexion, extension, abduction, adduction, circumduction (no axial rotation).
   - *Example:* Wrist joint (radiocarpal), metacarpophalangeal joints.
6. **Saddle / Sellar Joint (Biaxial with conjunct rotation):** Concavo-convex reciprocal articular surfaces.
   - *Example:* First carpometacarpal joint (base of thumb), sternoclavicular joint.
7. **Ball and Socket / Spheroidal Joint (Multiaxial):** Globular head fitting into a cup-shaped cavity; movements in infinite axes (flexion, extension, abduction, adduction, medial/lateral rotation, circumduction).
   - *Example:* Shoulder joint (glenohumeral), hip joint.

#### 2. Mention the histological differences between skeletal muscle and cardiac muscle. [5]

| Feature | Skeletal Muscle | Cardiac Muscle |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Location** | Attached to bones of skeleton | Myocardium of heart wall |
| **Fiber Shape & Size** | Long, cylindrical, unbranched fibers | Shorter, cylindrical, branched fibers forming a network |
| **Nucleus** | Multinucleated; peripheral, flat, oval | Single (rarely two); centrally located, oval |
| **Cross-Striations** | Prominent and well-defined | Present but less prominent than skeletal muscle |
| **Intercalated Discs** | Absent | Present (hallmark: junctional complexes with desmosomes and gap junctions) |
| **Sarcoplasmic Reticulum** | Well developed with terminal cisternae forming triads with T-tubules at A-I junction | Less developed; forms diadic contacts with T-tubules at Z-line |
| **Control** | Voluntary (somatic motor nervous system) | Involuntary (autonomic nervous system & conducting system) |
| **Fatigue & Action** | Rapid contraction, fatigues easily | Rhythmic, continuous, myogenic, fatigue-resistant |
| **Regeneration** | Limited (via satellite cells) | Virtually none (repairs via dense fibrous scar tissue) |

#### 3. Draw and label the histological diagram of spleen. [5]
*Histological Description & Key Diagram Components:*
- **Capsule & Trabeculae:** Covered by simple squamous mesothelium (peritoneum); dense irregular fibroelastic connective tissue capsule with smooth muscle fibers sending branching trabeculae into the splenic pulp.
- **White Pulp (Malpighian Corpuscles / Splenic Nodules):**
  - Composed of lymphoid tissue surrounding a **central artery** (eccentrically located arteriole).
  - **PALS (Periarteriolar Lymphoid Sheath):** Predominantly T-lymphocytes immediately surrounding the central arteriole.
  - **Splenic Lymphoid Nodules (Germinal Centers):** Rich in B-lymphocytes, displacing the central arteriole to the periphery.
  - **Marginal Zone:** Transition zone between white and red pulp, contains antigen-presenting cells, macrophages, and specialized B cells.
- **Red Pulp (Surrounding white pulp, fills ~75% of volume):**
  - **Splenic Cords (Cords of Billroth):** Reticular connective tissue meshwork packed with RBCs, macrophages, plasma cells, lymphocytes, and dendritic cells.
  - **Splenic Sinusoids (Venous Sinuses):** Broad, irregular vascular channels lined by elongated, rod-like endothelial cells (*stave cells*) separated by wide slit-like intercellular gaps resting on an incomplete, fenestrated basement membrane (barrel-hoop arrangement).

#### 4. Write short notes on: [3×3=9]
##### a. Neurulation
- **Definition:** Embryological process during the 3rd and 4th weeks of development by which the neural plate forms the neural tube, giving rise to the central nervous system.
- **Steps:**
  1. *Neural Induction:* Notochord and prechordal mesoderm secrete morphogens (chordin, noggin) that induce overlying embryonic ectoderm to thicken into the **neural plate**.
  2. *Neural Folds & Groove:* Lateral edges of neural plate elevate to form neural folds, with a depressed midline neural groove.
  3. *Closure:* Neural folds approach each other in the midline and fuse beginning in the cervical region (5th somite) progressing craniad and caudad, converting the groove into the **neural tube**.
  4. *Neuropores:* Anterior (cranial) neuropore closes on day 25 (lamina terminalis); posterior (caudal) neuropore closes on day 28.
- **Clinical Applied:** Failure of cranial neuropore closure causes **anencephaly**; failure of caudal neuropore closure leads to **spina bifida** (occulta, cystica, meningocele, myelomeningocele). Prophylaxis: Pre-conceptional folic acid.

##### b. Blood supply of typical long bone
Long bones receive blood from four distinct vascular systems:
1. **Nutrient Artery:** Enters through the nutrient foramen obliquely directed away from the growing end ("To the elbow I go, from the knee I flee"). Traverses cortex, enters medullary cavity, divides into ascending and descending branches providing ~70-80% of cortical and all marrow supply.
2. **Epiphyseal Arteries:** Derived from periarticular arterial anastomoses around joints; enter non-articular bony surfaces to supply the epiphysis.
3. **Metaphyseal Arteries:** Arise from systemic arteries near joint capsules; penetrate directly into metaphysis. Form hairpin loops adjacent to the epiphyseal plate. *Clinical note:* Sluggish blood flow in these loops makes the metaphysis the primary site of acute hematogenous osteomyelitis in children.
4. **Periosteal Arteries:** Numerous small vessels from overlying muscular arteries; supply the outer one-third of the compact cortex.

##### c. Gross features of palatine tonsil
- **Location:** Situated in the tonsillar fossa (lateral wall of oropharynx) between the palatoglossal arch (anteriorly) and palatopharyngeal arch (posteriorly).
- **External Features:** Two surfaces (medial and lateral), two borders (anterior and posterior), two poles (upper and lower).
  - *Medial surface:* Free, lined by non-keratinized stratified squamous epithelium; displays 12–15 **tonsillar crypts**, of which the largest is the *crypta magna* (intratonsillar cleft).
  - *Lateral (deep) surface:* Covered by a distinct fibrous capsule separated from the superior constrictor muscle by loose areolar peritonsillar space containing the paratonsillar vein (source of post-tonsillectomy bleeding).
  - *Upper pole:* Extends into soft palate; related to supratonsillar fossa.
  - *Lower pole:* Attached to the side of tongue; related to lingual tonsil.
- **Arterial Supply:** Tonsillar branch of facial artery (main), ascending palatine (facial), ascending pharyngeal, dorsal lingual, and descending palatine arteries.
- **Lymphatic Drainage:** Drains primarily into the **jugulodigastric (tonsillar) lymph node**, located below the angle of mandible.

#### 5. Mention the derivatives of branchial apparatus. [4+2=6]
The branchial (pharyngeal) apparatus consists of arches (mesoderm + neural crest), pouches (endoderm), grooves/clefts (ectoderm), and membranes.

1. **Pharyngeal Arches (Skeletal & Muscular Derivatives):**
   - **1st Arch (Mandibular - CN V3):**
     - *Skeletal:* Meckel's cartilage → malleus, incus, sphenomandibular ligament; mandible develops around Meckel's cartilage.
     - *Muscles:* Muscles of mastication (temporalis, masseter, medial and lateral pterygoids), mylohyoid, anterior belly of digastric, tensor veli palatini, tensor tympani.
   - **2nd Arch (Hyoid - CN VII):**
     - *Skeletal:* Reichert's cartilage → stapes, styloid process, stylohyoid ligament, lesser horn and upper body of hyoid.
     - *Muscles:* Muscles of facial expression, posterior belly of digastric, stylohyoid, stapedius.
   - **3rd Arch (CN IX):**
     - *Skeletal:* Greater horn and lower body of hyoid bone.
     - *Muscles:* Stylopharyngeus.
   - **4th Arch (CN X - Superior laryngeal nerve):**
     - *Skeletal:* Thyroid and epiglottic cartilages.
     - *Muscles:* Cricothyroid, levator veli palatini, pharyngeal constrictors.
   - **6th Arch (CN X - Recurrent laryngeal nerve):**
     - *Skeletal:* Cricoid, arytenoid, corniculate, cuneiform cartilages.
     - *Muscles:* All intrinsic muscles of larynx except cricothyroid.

2. **Pharyngeal Pouches (Endodermal Derivatives):**
   - *1st Pouch:* Tubotympanic recess (middle ear cavity, mastoid antrum, auditory/Eustachian tube).
   - *2nd Pouch:* Palatine tonsillar fossa, epithelium of palatine tonsils.
   - *3rd Pouch:* Inferior parathyroid gland (dorsal wing), thymus (ventral wing).
   - *4th Pouch:* Superior parathyroid gland (dorsal wing), ultimopharyngeal body / parafollicular C cells of thyroid (ventral wing).

3. **Pharyngeal Clefts & Membranes:**
   - *1st Cleft:* External acoustic meatus.
   - *2nd, 3rd, 4th Clefts:* Overgrown by 2nd arch forming cervical sinus (normally obliterates; persistent sinus forms cervical cyst/fistula).
   - *1st Membrane:* Tympanic membrane.

#### 6. Write down the boundaries and contents of axilla. [5]
The axilla is a pyramid-shaped space between the upper thoracic wall and upper arm:
- **Boundaries:**
  - **Apex (Cervico-axillary canal):** Directed upwards towards base of neck. Bounded anteriorly by clavicle (posterior border), medially by outer border of 1st rib, posteriorly by superior border of scapula.
  - **Base (Floor):** Directed downwards, formed by skin, subcutaneous tissue, and concave axillary fascia (supported by suspensory ligament of axilla).
  - **Anterior Wall:** Formed by pectoralis major, subclavius, pectoralis minor, and clavipectoral fascia.
  - **Posterior Wall:** Formed from above down by subscapularis, teres major, and latissimus dorsi.
  - **Medial Wall:** Formed by upper 4 to 5 ribs with their intercostal muscles, covered by upper digitations of serratus anterior.
  - **Lateral Wall:** Narrow; formed by intertubercular sulcus of humerus, coracobrachialis, and short head of biceps brachii.
- **Contents:**
  1. Axillary artery and its 6 branches.
  2. Axillary vein and its tributaries.
  3. Brachial plexus: Cords (lateral, medial, posterior) and their branches.
  4. Axillary lymph nodes (5 groups: anterior/pectoral, posterior/scapular, lateral, central, apical).
  5. Long thoracic nerve (of Bell) and intercostobrachial nerve.
  6. Axillary fat and loose areolar tissue enclosed within the axillary sheath.

#### 8. Write short notes on: [3×3=9]
##### a. Long saphenous vein (Great Saphenous Vein)
- **Origin & Course:** Originates on the dorsum of foot from the medial end of dorsal venous arch; ascends anterior to medial malleolus, passes upward along medial border of tibia, crosses behind medial condyle of femur, ascends anteromedially in thigh.
- **Termination:** Pierces the cribriform fascia at the **saphenous opening** (saphenous hiatus) in deep fascia to drain into the femoral vein, ~3-4 cm inferolateral to pubic tubercle.
- **Tributaries at saphenous opening:** Superficial epigastric, superficial circumflex iliac, superficial external pudendal veins.
- **Valves:** Contains 10–20 bicuspid valves (highest number in distal leg) to prevent retrograde flow.
- **Clinical Significance:**
  - *Varicose veins:* Incompetence of saphenofemoral junction valve or perforator valves leads to engorgement and tortuosity.
  - *Saphenous cutdown:* Accessible anterior to medial malleolus for emergency venous cutdown.
  - *Coronary Artery Bypass Graft (CABG):* Most commonly harvested autologous vascular graft.

##### b. Lacrimal apparatus
- **Components:**
  1. **Lacrimal Gland:** Lodged in lacrimal fossa on anterolateral roof of orbit; divided into larger orbital and smaller palpebral parts by the tendon of levator palpebrae superioris. Secretes tears via 10–12 ducts into superior conjunctival fornix.
  2. **Lacrimal Puncta & Canaliculi:** Tears sweep across cornea to medially situated superior and inferior puncta on eyelid margins, leading into superior and inferior lacrimal canaliculi.
  3. **Lacrimal Sac:** Lodged in lacrimal groove on medial orbital wall; upper blind end receives canaliculi.
  4. **Nasolacrimal Duct:** Membranous canal (~18 mm long) descending from lacrimal sac through bony canal to open into the **inferior meatus of nose**, guarded by the valve of Hasner (plica lacrimalis).
- **Secretomotor Innervation:** Parasympathetic preganglionic fibers from superior salivatory nucleus (CN VII) → greater petrosal nerve → nerve of pterygoid canal → pterygopalatine ganglion (synapse) → zygomatic nerve → zygomaticotemporal branch → lacrimal nerve → gland.

##### c. Anatomical snuffbox
- **Definition:** Triangular depression on the posterolateral aspect of the wrist visible when thumb is fully extended and abducted.
- **Boundaries:**
  - *Anterior (lateral):* Tendons of **Abductor pollicis longus** and **Extensor pollicis brevis**.
  - *Posterior (medial):* Tendon of **Extensor pollicis longus**.
  - *Floor:* Scaphoid bone (proximally), trapezium (distally), and base of 1st metacarpal bone.
  - *Roof:* Skin, superficial fascia containing cephalic vein and superficial branch of radial nerve.
- **Contents:**
  - **Radial artery:** Traverses the floor deep to tendons, passing from front of wrist to dorsal aspect before entering 1st interosseous space.
- **Clinical Significance:**
  - Tenderness in anatomical snuffbox is pathognomonic for **fracture of the scaphoid bone**; risk of avascular necrosis of proximal fragment due to retrograde blood supply.
  - Radial artery pulsations are readily palpable here.

### Section B [25 Marks]

#### 1. Describe the ionic basis of action potential in a nerve fibre with its well labeled diagram. [5]
An action potential is a rapid, transient, all-or-none reversal of the resting membrane potential (RMP) propagating along an excitable membrane.
- **Resting Membrane Potential (~ -70 mV to -90 mV):** Maintained by Na⁺/K⁺ ATPase pump (pumps 3 Na⁺ out for 2 K⁺ in) and high resting permeability to K⁺ via K⁺ leak channels.
- **Phases and Ionic Basis:**
  1. **Depolarization Phase:**
     - A stimulus brings membrane potential to threshold (~ -55 mV).
     - Voltage-gated Na⁺ channels open rapidly (activation 'm' gates open).
     - Massive influx of Na⁺ down its electrochemical gradient leads to rapid depolarization, overshooting 0 mV up to approximately +35 mV.
  2. **Repolarization Phase:**
     - Voltage-gated Na⁺ channels close rapidly by closure of inactivation 'h' gates (inactivated state).
     - Voltage-gated K⁺ channels open slowly.
     - Outflux of K⁺ restores electronegativity inside the cell, bringing the potential back towards RMP.
  3. **Hyperpolarization (After-hyperpolarization):**
     - K⁺ channels are slow to close, allowing continued K⁺ efflux, briefly driving the potential more negative than normal RMP (~ -90 mV).
  4. **Restoration of RMP:**
     - Closure of voltage-gated K⁺ channels and active pumping by Na⁺/K⁺ ATPase restores baseline ion distributions.
- *Refractory Periods:*
  - *Absolute Refractory Period (ARP):* From threshold to early repolarization; Na⁺ channels are inactivated. No second action potential possible.
  - *Relative Refractory Period (RRP):* During late repolarization/hyperpolarization; some Na⁺ channels reset; supranormal stimulus can trigger an action potential.

#### 3. Explain the basis of classification of human beings into Rh system. Add a note on its importance in blood transfusion. [2+3=5]
- **Basis of Rh System Classification:**
  - Based on the presence or absence of the **Rh (Rhesus) antigens** on the red blood cell surface.
  - Although there are 5 major antigens (C, D, E, c, e), the **D-antigen** is by far the most immunogenic and clinically significant.
  - **Rh-Positive (Rh+):** Individuals whose RBCs possess the D-antigen (~85% of Caucasian, >95% of Asian populations).
  - **Rh-Negative (Rh-):** Individuals lacking the D-antigen (genotype *dd*).
  - Unlike the ABO system, natural anti-D antibodies do NOT normally exist in the plasma; anti-D (IgG) is formed only upon immune exposure (sensitization via transfusion or pregnancy).
- **Importance in Blood Transfusion & Clinical Medicine:**
  1. *Transfusion Compatibility:* Rh-negative individuals must always receive Rh-negative blood. Transfusing Rh-positive blood into an Rh-negative person stimulates anti-D antibody production. A subsequent Rh+ transfusion causes acute immune hemolysis, shock, renal failure, and death.
  2. *Erythroblastosis Fetalis (Hemolytic Disease of the Newborn - HDN):*
     - Occurs when an Rh-negative mother carries an Rh-positive fetus.
     - During 1st delivery, fetal Rh+ RBCs enter maternal circulation (fetomaternal hemorrhage), sensitizing mother to produce anti-Rh antibodies (first IgM, then class-switched to IgG).
     - In subsequent pregnancies with an Rh+ fetus, maternal anti-D IgG crosses placenta into fetal circulation, destroying fetal RBCs.
     - Consequences: Severe hemolytic anemia, jaundice, kernicterus (bilirubin deposition in basal ganglia), hepatosplenomegaly, or hydrops fetalis.
     - *Prevention:* Administer anti-D immunoglobulin (RhoGAM) to Rh-negative mothers within 72 hours of delivering an Rh-positive baby, or at 28 weeks of gestation, neutralizing fetal D-antigen before maternal sensitization occurs.

#### 4. Write short notes on: [3×3=9]
##### a. Negative Feedback Mechanism
- **Definition:** A primary homeostatic control mechanism in which the biological system responds to a perturbation in a direction opposite to the initiating stimulus, restoring variable to its normal set-point.
- **Components:** (1) *Sensor/Receptor:* Detects change in regulated variable. (2) *Integrator/Control Center:* Compares detected signal with set point. (3) *Effector:* Alters activity to reverse deviation.
- **Examples:**
  - *Blood Pressure Regulation:* Elevated BP stimulates arterial baroreceptors (carotid sinus, aortic arch) → signals medulla → increases parasympathetic output, decreases sympathetic outflow → vasodilation and decreased heart rate → BP drops back to normal.
  - *Endocrine Regulation:* Elevated thyroid hormones (T3, T4) inhibit secretion of TRH from hypothalamus and TSH from anterior pituitary, preventing hormone excess.

##### b. Functions of plasma proteins
Normal concentration is 6–8 g/dL (Albumin ~3.5–5.0, Globulin ~2.0–3.5, Fibrinogen ~0.2–0.4 g/dL).
1. **Colloid Osmotic (Oncotic) Pressure:** Albumin contributes ~80% of total plasma oncotic pressure (~25 mmHg), opposing hydrostatic pressure and maintaining intravascular fluid volume (deficiency causes generalized edema).
2. **Immunity & Defense:** Gamma-globulins act as immunoglobulins (IgA, IgG, IgM, IgD, IgE) combating bacterial and viral infections; complement proteins mediate immune lysis.
3. **Coagulation & Hemostasis:** Fibrinogen and prothrombin are essential clotting factors preventing fatal hemorrhage.
4. **Transport Function:** Albumin binds and transports bilirubin, free fatty acids, calcium, thyroid hormones, and drugs. Transferrin transports iron; ceruloplasmin transports copper.
5. **Buffering Capacity:** Account for ~15% of blood buffering capacity; amphoteric nature allows binding of H⁺.
6. **Viscosity & Blood Pressure:** Fibrinogen and globulins contribute to blood viscosity, maintaining peripheral vascular resistance.

##### c. Neutrophils
- **Characteristics:** Most abundant circulating white blood cells (50–70% of total differential count; 2,000–7,000/μL); lifespan 6–8 hours in circulation, 1–2 days in tissue.
- **Morphology:** Multilobed nucleus (3–5 lobes connected by thin chromatin strands; polymorphonuclear), cytoplasm contains pale pink/lilac granules (azurophilic primary granules with myeloperoxidase, specific secondary granules with lysozyme and lactoferrin).
- **Functions:**
  - *First line of cellular defense* against acute bacterial and fungal infections.
  - *Chemotaxis:* Directed migration toward infection site responding to chemoattractants (C5a, LTB4, bacterial peptides).
  - *Phagocytosis:* Engulfment of opsonized pathogens (opsonized with IgG, C3b).
  - *Microbicidal Killing:* Oxygen-dependent (respiratory burst via NADPH oxidase producing O₂⁻, H₂O₂, and hypochlorous acid via myeloperoxidase) and oxygen-independent mechanisms (defensins, lysozyme).
  - *NETs (Neutrophil Extracellular Traps):* Release web-like chromatin-granule networks trapping pathogens.

### Section C [25 Marks]

#### 5. Define synapse. Write the steps of synaptic transmission with diagram. [1+5=6]
- **Definition:** A synapse is an anatomically specialized junction between two excitable cells (neuron to neuron, or neuron to effector cell) across which electrical signals are transmitted, predominantly through chemical neurotransmitters.
- **Steps of Chemical Synaptic Transmission:**
  1. *Action Potential Arrival:* Nerve action potential propagates down axon and invades presynaptic terminal bouton.
  2. *Depolarization & Ca²⁺ Influx:* Presynaptic membrane depolarization activates voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels; Ca²⁺ rushes into terminal down steep concentration gradient.
  3. *Vesicle Fusion & Exocytosis:* Intracellular Ca²⁺ binds to **synaptotagmin**, triggering SNARE complex proteins (synaptobrevin, syntaxin, SNAP-25) to mediate docking and fusion of synaptic vesicles with presynaptic membrane, releasing neurotransmitter into synaptic cleft.
  4. *Diffusion across Cleft:* Neurotransmitter molecules diffuse across the narrow (~20–40 nm) synaptic cleft.
  5. *Postsynaptic Receptor Binding:* Neurotransmitter binds specifically to postsynaptic ligand-gated ion channels (ionotropic) or G-protein coupled receptors (metabotropic).
  6. *Postsynaptic Potential Generation:*
     - Excitatory (e.g., Glutamate, ACh): Na⁺ or Ca²⁺ influx produces **EPSP** (Excitatory Postsynaptic Potential).
     - Inhibitory (e.g., GABA, Glycine): Cl⁻ influx or K⁺ efflux produces **IPSP** (Inhibitory Postsynaptic Potential).
  7. *Signal Termination:* Neurotransmitter action terminated rapidly by enzymatic degradation (e.g., Acetylcholinesterase), presynaptic reuptake (e.g., serotonin, dopamine), or glial uptake.

#### 7. Classify leucocytes. Write down the stages of erythropoiesis. [2+3=5]
- **Classification of Leucocytes (WBCs — Normal count: 4,000–11,000/μL):**
  1. **Granulocytes (Polymorphonuclear leucocytes):** Possess prominent lobed nuclei and specific cytoplasmic granules.
     - *Neutrophils (50–70%):* Neutral lilac granules; phagocytosis of bacteria.
     - *Eosinophils (1–4%):* Coarse brick-red granules (major basic protein); allergic responses and defense against helminthic parasites.
     - *Basophils (0.5–1%):* Coarse dark-blue/purple granules (histamine, heparin); immediate hypersensitivity.
  2. **Agranulocytes (Mononuclear leucocytes):** Lack specific granules; non-lobed nuclei.
     - *Lymphocytes (20–40%):* Large round nucleus with narrow rim of blue cytoplasm; specific adaptive immunity (T-cells, B-cells, NK cells).
     - *Monocytes (2–8%):* Largest WBC, kidney/horseshoe-shaped nucleus; precursors of tissue macrophages.
- **Stages of Erythropoiesis (RBC Development):**
  Takes ~7 days in red bone marrow; regulated by Erythropoietin (EPO) from kidneys:
  1. **Pluripotent Hemopoietic Stem Cell (PHSC)** → Myeloid stem cell (CFU-GEMM) → BFU-E → CFU-E.
  2. **Proerythroblast (Pronormoblast):** Large cell (14–20 μm), deep basophilic cytoplasm, large nucleus with prominent nucleoli; no hemoglobin.
  3. **Early Normoblast (Basophilic Erythroblast):** Condensed chromatin, intense basophilia due to abundant RNA/ribosomes; hemoglobin synthesis begins.
  4. **Intermediate Normoblast (Polychromatophilic Erythroblast):** Cytoplasm shows mixed staining (polychromasia: blue ribosomes + pink hemoglobin); last stage capable of mitosis.
  5. **Late Normoblast (Orthochromatophilic Erythroblast):** Cytoplasm almost completely acidophilic/pink; pyknotic nucleus that is extruded at the end of this stage.
  6. **Reticulocyte:** Non-nucleated immature RBC containing residual reticular network of ribosomal RNA (precipitated by supra-vital stains like brilliant cresyl blue); enters circulation; matures in 24–48 hours (normal count 0.5–2%).
  7. **Mature Erythrocyte:** Biconcave, enucleated disc (7.2 μm diameter, lifespan ~120 days).

#### 8. Write short notes on: [3×3=9]
##### a. Role of platelets in hemostasis
Platelets (150,000–450,000/μL) are essential for primary hemostasis:
1. *Adhesion:* Endothelial damage exposes subendothelial collagen. Platelets adhere via glycoprotein receptors (Gp Ib-IX-V) binding to **von Willebrand Factor (vWF)**.
2. *Activation & Shape Change:* Platelets transform from discoid to spiny spheres with pseudopodia; release cytoplasmic granules (dense granules release ADP, Ca²⁺, serotonin; alpha granules release fibrinogen, vWF, PDGF).
3. *Aggregation:* Synthesized **Thromboxane A2 (TxA2)** and released ADP stimulate Gp IIb/IIIa receptors to cross-link with circulating fibrinogen, forming a **temporary primary hemostatic plug**.
4. *Coagulation acceleration:* Provide negative phospholipid membrane surface (platelet factor 3) for assembly of tenase and prothrombinase complexes, generating thrombin.
5. *Clot Retraction:* Platelet actin and myosin contract, tightening the clot and expressing serum.

##### b. Landsteiner's Law
Formulated by Karl Landsteiner in 1900 for the ABO blood grouping:
- **First Part:** If an agglutinogen (antigen) is present on the red blood cell membrane of an individual, the corresponding agglutinin (antibody) MUST be absent in the plasma.
- **Second Part:** If an agglutinogen is absent on the red blood cell membrane, the corresponding agglutinin MUST be present in the plasma.
- *Application:*
  - Blood Group A: Antigen A present → Anti-B antibody in plasma.
  - Blood Group B: Antigen B present → Anti-A antibody in plasma.
  - Blood Group AB: Both A and B present → Neither antibody present.
  - Blood Group O: Neither antigen present → Both Anti-A and Anti-B present.
- *Note:* The second part of Landsteiner's law strictly applies to the **ABO system**, and does NOT apply to the **Rh system** (anti-D is not naturally present in Rh-negative blood).

##### c. Refractory Period
- **Definition:** The brief period of time following an action potential during which an excitable cell (nerve or muscle) exhibits reduced or absent responsiveness to a second stimulus.
- **Subtypes:**
  1. *Absolute Refractory Period (ARP):*
     - Time interval from the start of action potential until early repolarization is achieved.
     - Voltage-gated Na⁺ channels are completely inactivated (closed inactivation 'h' gates).
     - No stimulus, regardless of strength, can evoke a second action potential.
     - *Significance:* Sets upper limit on maximum firing frequency; ensures unidirectional forward propagation of action potentials.
  2. *Relative Refractory Period (RRP):*
     - Follows ARP during late repolarization and after-hyperpolarization.
     - Some Na⁺ channels have transitioned back to the resting closed state; high K⁺ permeability hyperpolarizes membrane.
     - A stronger-than-normal (suprathreshold) stimulus can elicit an action potential (though of smaller amplitude).

##### a. Features of autosomal dominant mode of inheritance
1. *Phenotypic Expression:* Expressed in both heterozygotes (*Aa*) and homozygotes (*AA*); one mutated copy of the gene is sufficient to cause the trait.
2. *Vertical Transmission:* Trait appears in every generation without skipping; every affected individual has at least one affected parent (unless resulting from a *de novo* mutation).
3. *Recurrence Risk:* An affected heterozygous parent has a 50% (1 in 2) probability of passing the mutant allele to each offspring, regardless of biological sex.
4. *Equal Sex Distribution:* Males and females are equally affected and equally likely to transmit the condition (male-to-male transmission occurs, ruling out X-linkage).
5. *Variable Expressivity & Incomplete Penetrance:* Clinical severity may vary widely between family members; some carriers may show no overt signs despite carrying the gene (reduced penetrance).
6. *Examples:* Achondroplasia, Marfan syndrome, Huntington disease, Neurofibromatosis type 1, Dentinogenesis imperfecta.

### Section "B" [25 marks]

#### 1. What is the composition of enamel? Discuss Amelogenesis in detail. [1+5=6]
- **Composition of Enamel:** Enamel is the hardest and most mineralized biological tissue in the human body:
  - *Inorganic matter (96% by weight, 86–89% by volume):* Primarily calcium hydroxyapatite crystals $[Ca_{10}(PO_4)_6(OH)_2]$ with trace minerals (carbonate, magnesium, fluoride, sodium).
  - *Organic matter (1% by weight, 2% by volume):* Non-collagenous proteins: **Amelogenins** (90% of developing enamel, regulate crystal spacing), **Enamelins**, **Ameloblastin**, and **Tuftelin**.
  - *Water (3% by weight, 9–12% by volume):* Trapped in inter-crystalline spaces and hydration shells.
- **Amelogenesis (Enamel Formation):**
  Synthesized by ameloblasts (derived from inner enamel epithelium); occurs in two main phases: matrix secretion and mineralization/maturation.
  1. *Morphogenetic & Organizing Stage:* Inner enamel epithelial (IEE) cells differentiate from cuboidal to tall columnar pre-ameloblasts; nucleus shifts towards stratum intermedium (reversal of functional polarity). Pre-ameloblasts induce underlying dental papilla cells to differentiate into odontoblasts.
  2. *Secretory Stage:* Odontoblasts form predentin, which triggers ameloblasts to secrete initial structureless enamel matrix. Ameloblasts develop a conical apical projection, **Tomes' process**, which dictates the formation of rod (prism) and inter-rod enamel.
  3. *Maturation Stage:* After full thickness of enamel is laid down, ameloblasts lose Tomes' processes and modulate between **ruffle-ended** (pumping mineral ions: Ca²⁺ and phosphate into matrix) and **smooth-ended** (resorbing water and degraded amelogenin proteins via MMP-20 and kallikrein-4). Hydroxyapatite crystals grow in width and thickness.
  4. *Protective Stage:* Ameloblasts regress to cuboidal cells, uniting with stratum intermedium, stellate reticulum, and outer enamel epithelium to form the **Reduced Enamel Epithelium (REE)**, protecting enamel until tooth eruption.

#### 2. Describe in detail the morphology of permanent maxillary left canine with well labeled diagram. [5]
- **General Features:** Tooth #23 (FDI 23 / Universal 11). "Cornerstone of the dental arch"; longest tooth in human dentition (crown ~10 mm, root ~17 mm, total ~27 mm); exhibits canine eminence in alveolar bone.
- **Morphological Aspects:**
  1. *Labial Aspect:* Pentagonal outline. Single prominent cusp with two sloping ridges: **mesial cusp ridge is shorter** than the distal cusp ridge. Labial surface shows a marked **labial ridge** dividing it into shallow mesiolabial and distolabial developmental depressions. Mesial contact area is at junction of incisal and middle thirds; distal contact area is more cervical (middle of middle third).
  2. *Lingual Aspect:* Displays a well-developed, bulky **cingulum** centered in the cervical third. Distinct **lingual ridge** extends from cusp tip to cingulum, flanked by mesial and distal lingual fossae. Margins defined by mesial and distal marginal ridges.
  3. *Mesial Aspect:* Wedge/triangular shape with a broad labiolingual dimension at cervical third, providing immense masticatory resistance. Incisal cusp tip is positioned labial to the long axis of the root.
  4. *Distal Aspect:* Similar to mesial, but the cervical line (CEJ) exhibits less curvature towards the cusp tip (~1 mm vs ~2.5 mm on mesial).
  5. *Incisal Aspect:* Diamond/asymmetrical shape. Labiolingual dimension significantly exceeds mesiodistal dimension. Cusp tip located slightly labial and mesial to center.
  6. *Root:* Single, conical, exceptionally long with developmental depressions on mesial and distal surfaces; apex tilts slightly distally.

#### 3. Discuss the functions of pulp. Add a note on Plexus of Raschkow. [3+2=5]
- **Functions of Dental Pulp:**
  1. *Inductive:* Directs early development of dental lamina and initiates enamel organ formation; odontoblasts induce amelogenesis.
  2. *Formative (Dentinogenic):* Odontoblasts continuously synthesize and deposit dentin (primary dentin during development, secondary dentin throughout life, and tertiary/reparative dentin in response to trauma/caries).
  3. *Nutritive:* Pulp capillary microcirculation supplies nutrients and oxygen to active odontoblasts and their cytoplasmic processes inside dentinal tubules.
  4. *Sensory / Protective:* Rich sensory innervation transmits all stimuli (thermal, mechanical, chemical, tactile) exclusively as **pain sensation** via A-delta (sharp, rapid pain) and C-fibers (dull, throbbing, persistent ache).
  5. *Defensive / Reparative:* Mobilizes inflammatory cells (macrophages, lymphocytes) and deposits tertiary calcified dentin bridges isolating pulp from bacterial noxious insults.
- **Plexus of Raschkow (Subodontoblastic Nerve Plexus):**
  - Located in the **cell-free zone of Weil** immediately beneath the odontoblast layer in the coronal pulp (absent in root pulp until complete root formation).
  - Composed of a dense intertwining plexus of myelinated (A-beta, A-delta) and unmyelinated (C) sensory nerve fibers derived from the mandibular and maxillary divisions of the trigeminal nerve (CN V).
  - Fibers lose their myelin sheaths as they pass through the cell-rich zone into the cell-free zone; terminal unmyelinated twigs terminate around odontoblast cell bodies or enter the inner 100–200 μm of dentinal tubules, playing a central role in dentinal pain transmission.

#### 4. Write short notes on: [3×3=9]
##### a. Line angles and point angles
- *Line Angle:* Junction formed by the meeting of two planar tooth crown surfaces. Named by combining the two surfaces:
  - *Anterior teeth (6 line angles):* Mesiolabial, distolabial, mesiolingual, distolingual, labioincisal, linguoincisal.
  - *Posterior teeth (8 line angles):* Mesiobuccal, distobuccal, mesiolingual, distolingual, bucco-occlusal, linguo-occlusal, mesio-occlusal, disto-occlusal.
- *Point Angle:* Junction formed by the confluence of three planar tooth crown surfaces.
  - *Anterior teeth (4 point angles):* Mesiolabioincisal, distolabioincisal, mesiolinguoincisal, distolinguoincisal.
  - *Posterior teeth (4 point angles):* Mesiobucco-occlusal, distobucco-occlusal, mesiolinguo-occlusal, distolinguo-occlusal.

##### b. Hypocalcified structures of enamel
These are developmental areas containing higher protein/water concentration and less mineralized hydroxyapatite:
1. *Enamel Lamellae:* Thin, leaf-like organic structures extending longitudinally from the outer enamel surface down to the dentinoenamel junction (DEJ) or into dentin. Represent uncalcified matrix or cracks filled with organic debris. Potential pathway for caries invasion.
2. *Enamel Tufts:* Microscopic, ribbon-like, brush- or grass-tuft structures extending from the DEJ into the inner one-third of the enamel. Rich in tuftelin; represent hypomineralized enamel rods and inter-rod substance.
3. *Enamel Spindles:* Odontoblastic process extensions that crossed the DEJ into the pre-enamel epithelium before amelogenesis and became entrapped in calcified enamel; abundant beneath cusps; clinically hypersensitive.

##### c. Elevated landmarks of tooth
1. *Cusp:* A pyramidal elevation on the occlusal surface of premolars and molars and the incisal edge of canines.
2. *Tubercle:* A small elevation on some part of the crown produced by an extra formation of enamel (e.g., Cusp of Carabelli on the mesiopalatal cusp of permanent maxillary first molar).
3. *Cingulum:* The lingual/palatal developmental lobe of anterior teeth in the cervical third.
4. *Ridge:* Linear elevation named according to location: Marginal ridges, Triangular ridges, Transverse ridges, Oblique ridges, Labial/Buccal ridges.
5. *Mamelons:* Three rounded protuberances on the incisal ridges of newly erupted permanent incisors.

#### 5. Enumerate the morphological stages of tooth development. Discuss about transient structures. [1+4=5]
- **Morphological Stages:**
  1. Dental Lamina & Bud stage (initiation/proliferation)
  2. Cap stage (proliferation/histodifferentiation)
  3. Early Bell stage (histodifferentiation/morphodifferentiation)
  4. Advanced Bell stage (apposition of enamel and dentin)
  5. Root formation stage
- **Transient Structures of Tooth Development:**
  Temporary embryological epithelial structures present during the cap and early bell stages that disappear before hard tissue formation; act as signaling centers:
  1. *Enamel Knot:* A localized, non-dividing cluster of densely packed epithelial cells in the central inner enamel epithelium during late bud to cap stage. Acts as the **primary signaling center** producing morphogens (FGF-4, BMP-2, BMP-4, Shh, Wnt) that direct folding of the enamel organ and crown shape morphogenesis. Secondary enamel knots form at future cusp tips. Undergoes apoptosis.
  2. *Enamel Cord:* A vertical strand of condensed epithelial cells extending from the enamel knot across the stellate reticulum to the outer enamel epithelium. Acts as a mechanical anchor or reservoir.
  3. *Enamel Navel (Niche):* An invagination of the outer enamel epithelium where the enamel cord meets the surface, giving the false microscopic impression of a double attachment.
  4. *Dental Lamina Lateral Flap & Glands of Serres:* Rest of dental lamina disintegrates via mesenchymal invasion; remnants persist as isolated islands/pearls (**rests of Serres**), which can form odontogenic cysts.

#### 6. Classify salivary glands. Differentiate between serous and mucous acini. [2+3=5]
- **Classification of Salivary Glands:**
  - *By Size:*
    1. **Major Salivary Glands:** Paired, encapsulated, located outside oral cavity with long excretory ducts: Parotid (purely serous), Submandibular (mixed, predominantly serous ~80%), Sublingual (mixed, predominantly mucous).
    2. **Minor Salivary Glands:** Non-encapsulated, scattered throughout oral submucosa (~600–1000): Labial, buccal, palatine (purely mucous), lingual (von Ebner's: purely serous; Blandin-Nuhn: mixed).
  - *By Secretion Type:* Purely serous, Purely mucous, Mixed (seromucous).

- **Histological Differences between Serous and Mucous Acini:**

| Feature | Serous Acinus | Mucous Acinus |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Secretory Product** | Thin, watery, enzyme-rich (salivary amylase/ptyalin, lysozyme) | Thick, viscous, mucin-rich (lubricating glycoproteins) |
| **Acinus Shape & Lumen** | Small, rounded acinus with a narrow, indistinct central lumen | Larger, tubular/alveolar with a distinct, wider central lumen |
| **Cell Morphology** | Pyramidal cells with distinct boundaries | Columnar to cuboidal cells with less distinct cell boundaries |
| **Nucleus** | Spherical, round; situated in basal one-third; open chromatin | Flattened, compressed against the basal cell membrane |
| **Cytoplasm & Staining** | Intensely basophilic basal cytoplasm (abundant rough ER); dark zymogen granules apically; stains darkly with H&E | Pale, vacuolated, foamy appearance (mucin droplets wash out during H&E staining); PAS positive |
| **Special Associations** | Drains via long intercalated ducts; can cap mucous tubules as **serous demilunes of Giannuzzi** | Directly connects to striated ducts; short or absent intercalated ducts |

#### 7. Enlist the layers of dental apparatus. Add a note on reciprocal induction and describe the process of formation of root. [1+2+3=6]
- **Layers of the Enamel Organ (Bell Stage):**
  1. *Outer Enamel Epithelium (OEE):* Convex layer of cuboidal cells.
  2. *Stellate Reticulum:* Star-shaped cells connected by desmosomes in a glycosaminoglycan-rich extracellular matrix (mechanical cushion).
  3. *Stratum Intermedium:* 2–3 layers of squamous cells with high alkaline phosphatase activity (mineralization support).
  4. *Inner Enamel Epithelium (IEE):* Columnar cells that differentiate into ameloblasts.
- **Reciprocal Induction:**
  The mutual embryological communication between ectoderm (IEE) and ectomesenchyme (dental papilla):
  1. IEE cells differentiate into pre-ameloblasts and secrete signaling molecules that induce adjacent dental papilla cells to differentiate into **odontoblasts**.
  2. Odontoblasts deposit the first layer of predentin.
  3. The presence of newly formed dentin matrix physically triggers pre-ameloblasts to complete differentiation into secretory **ameloblasts** and begin secreting enamel matrix. (Without dentin, enamel cannot form; without IEE signaling, odontoblasts cannot differentiate).
- **Process of Root Formation:**
  1. Root development begins after crown formation reaches the cementoenamel junction (CEJ).
  2. *Hertwig's Epithelial Root Sheath (HERS):* Formed by the downward elongation and fusion of the Outer Enamel Epithelium and Inner Enamel Epithelium (without intervening stellate reticulum or stratum intermedium) at the cervical loop.
  3. HERS bends horizontally at the root base to form the **epithelial diaphragm**, enclosing the primary apical foramen.
  4. The inner layer of HERS induces adjacent dental papilla ectomesenchyme to differentiate into **radicular odontoblasts**, which form root dentin.
  5. Once root dentin is formed, HERS breaks down and fragments into the **epithelial rests of Malassez** in the periodontal ligament.
  6. Dental follicle cells contact the newly exposed root dentin, differentiate into **cementoblasts**, and deposit **cementum**.
  7. Multi-rooted teeth develop via ingrowth of tongue-like epithelial flaps from the epithelial diaphragm dividing the common trunk into multiple root canals.

#### 8. Write short notes on: [3×3=9]
##### a. Tooth numbering systems
1. **FDI Two-Digit System (ISO 3950):** First digit indicates quadrant (1–4 for permanent, 5–8 for primary); second digit indicates tooth position from midline (1–8 for permanent, 1–5 for primary). *Example: Permanent maxillary right central incisor = 11; primary mandibular left canine = 73.*
2. **Zsigmondy-Palmer System:** Uses a right-angled quadrant grid ($\llcorner, \lrcorner, \ulcorner, \urcorner$); permanent teeth numbered 1–8 from midline; primary teeth labeled A–E. *Example: $\underline{1\vert}$ = maxillary right central incisor.*
3. **Universal (American) Numbering System:** Permanent teeth numbered sequentially 1–32 starting from maxillary right 3rd molar (1) to maxillary left 3rd molar (16), down to mandibular left 3rd molar (17) around to mandibular right 3rd molar (32). Primary teeth designated by letters A through T.

##### b. Occlusal aspect of maxillary first premolar
- Hexagonal outline; buccolingual dimension is distinctly wider than mesiodistal dimension.
- Possesses two distinct cusps: **Buccal cusp** (larger, higher, sharper) and **Lingual cusp** (shorter by ~1 mm, blunter, tilted mesially).
- Distinct **Central Groove** runs mesiodistally, crossing the mesial marginal ridge as the characteristic **Mesial Marginal Developmental Groove** (crucial diagnostic hallmark distinguishing it from the 2nd premolar).
- Deep **Mesial concavity** on crown extending onto the root trunk.
- Two triangular fossae: Mesial and distal triangular fossae containing developmental pits.
- Usually has **two roots** (buccal and palatal) and two root canals.

##### c. Formation and secretion of saliva
- *Two-Stage Hypothesis of Saliva Secretion (Thaysen):*
  1. **Primary Secretion (Acini):**
     - Acinar cells synthesize and package proteins (amylase, mucins) into secretory granules, released via exocytosis upon autonomic stimulation.
     - Transport of Cl⁻ and water produces an **isotonic primary fluid** with electrolyte concentrations closely resembling blood plasma (high Na⁺, high Cl⁻, low K⁺, isotonic).
  2. **Secondary Modification (Duct System):**
     - As primary saliva flows through intercalated and striated ducts, ductal epithelium actively **reabsorbs Na⁺ and Cl⁻** and **secretes K⁺ and HCO₃⁻**.
     - Ductal cells are impermeable to water; therefore, net reabsorption of ions exceeds water absorption, converting saliva into a **hypotonic final fluid**.
  3. *Flow-Rate Dependence:* At high salivary flow rates, less time is available for ductal modification, so salivary Na⁺ and Cl⁻ concentrations rise and approach isotonic levels.

## Documents 12 to 15 — End Semester Papers (Biochemistry, Microbiology, Pharmacology, Pathology)

### Document 12 — Biochemistry (End Semester Examination)
- **Carbohydrates:** Polyhydroxy aldehydes or ketones. Classified into Monosaccharides (glucose, fructose), Disaccharides (sucrose, lactose, maltose), Oligosaccharides (3–10 units), Polysaccharides: Homopolysaccharides (starch, glycogen, cellulose) vs. Heteropolysaccharides (glycosaminoglycans/GAGs: hyaluronic acid, heparin, chondroitin sulfate).
- **Proteins:** Amino acid classification based on R-group (aliphatic, aromatic, basic, acidic, sulfur-containing). Structural organization: Primary (peptide bonds), Secondary ($\alpha$-helix, $\beta$-sheet via hydrogen bonds), Tertiary (3D folding via hydrophobic, ionic, disulfide bonds), Quaternary (multiple polypeptide chains like hemoglobin).
- **Lipids & Phospholipids:** Esters of fatty acids and alcohol. Phospholipids (glycerophospholipids like lecithin, sphingophospholipids like sphingomyelin) form cellular lipid bilayers, lung surfactant, and bile emulsifiers.
- **Enzyme Inhibition:** Competitive (resembles substrate, binds active site; $V_{\max}$ unchanged, $K_m$ increases; e.g., Statins inhibiting HMG-CoA reductase, Methotrexate inhibiting DHFR) vs. Non-competitive (binds allosteric site; $V_{\max}$ decreases, $K_m$ unchanged; e.g., Cyanide inhibiting cytochrome oxidase).
- **HMP Shunt:** Cytosolic pathway; oxidative phase generates **NADPH** (fatty acid synthesis, glutathione reduction) and non-oxidative phase generates **Ribose-5-phosphate** (nucleotide synthesis).
- **Heme Synthesis & Porphyrias:** Pathway begins in mitochondria with Glycine + Succinyl-CoA via rate-limiting **ALA synthase** (Vit B6-dependent) $\rightarrow$ Porphobilinogen $\rightarrow$ Uroporphyrinogen $\rightarrow$ Coproporphyrinogen $\rightarrow$ Protoporphyrin IX + $\text{Fe}^{2+} \rightarrow$ Heme. Defects cause Porphyrias; Acute Intermittent Porphyria (AIP) is due to porphobilinogen deaminase deficiency, manifesting as neuropsychiatric attacks and colicky abdominal pain without cutaneous photosensitivity.

### Document 13 — Microbiology (End Semester Examination)
- **Sterilization & Autoclave:** Sterilization kills all viable microorganisms including bacterial spores. Autoclave works on **moist heat under pressure** (steam under pressure): 121°C at 15 psi for 15 minutes. Latent heat of steam penetrates materials and denatures essential bacterial proteins. Uses: Surgical instruments, culture media, dressings. Control: *Geobacillus stearothermophilus* spores.
- **Culture Media:** Basal (nutrient agar), Enriched (blood agar, chocolate agar), Selective (MacConkey agar, Lowenstein-Jensen), Differential (MacConkey), Transport (Stuart's, Cary-Blair).
- **Kirby-Bauer Disk Diffusion:** Standardized inoculum of bacteria ($0.5\text{ McFarland}$) swabbed onto Mueller-Hinton agar; antibiotic disks applied; incubated at 37°C for 18–24 h; zones of inhibition measured with calipers and interpreted as Susceptible (S), Intermediate (I), or Resistant (R) according to CLSI guidelines.
- **Short notes:**
  - *Louis Pasteur:* Disproved spontaneous generation, developed pasteurization, rabies and anthrax vaccines, coined the term "vaccine".
  - *Bacterial Growth Curve:* Lag phase, Log (exponential) phase, Stationary phase, Decline (death) phase.
  - *Normal Flora:* Commensal microbes preventing pathogen colonization, synthesizing vitamins (K, B12), priming immunity.
- **Parasitology & Mycology:**
  - *Malaria Lab Diagnosis:* Thick blood film (detection/screening), thin blood film (species identification and quantification) stained with Giemsa/Leishman; Rapid Diagnostic Tests (RDTs detecting HRP-2 or pLDH); quantitative buffy coat (QBC), PCR.
  - *Wuchereria bancrofti:* Transmitted by *Culex* mosquito; microfilariae exhibit nocturnal periodicity in peripheral blood (sample collected between 10 PM and 2 AM); adult worms block lymphatics causing elephantiasis and hydrocele.
  - *Histoplasma capsulatum:* Thermal dimorphic fungus (mold in soil at 25°C with tuberculate macroconidia, intracellular yeast in macrophages at 37°C); diagnosed by Giemsa/GMS stain of bone marrow/sputum, fungal culture on SDA, and urine histoplasma antigen test.

### Document 14 — Pharmacology (End Semester Examination)
- **First-Pass Metabolism:** Pre-systemic metabolism of orally administered drugs in the gut wall or liver before reaching systemic circulation, drastically reducing bioavailability (e.g., Nitroglycerin, Propranolol, Lidocaine, Morphine).
- **Key Concepts:**
  - *Prodrug:* Inactive precursor metabolized in vivo into active drug (e.g., Enalapril $\rightarrow$ Enalaprilat, Levodopa $\rightarrow$ Dopamine).
  - *Plasma half-life ($t_{1/2}$):* Time taken for plasma drug concentration to decrease by 50% ($t_{1/2} = 0.693 \times V_d / CL$). Determines dosing interval and time to steady state (~4–5 half-lives).
  - *Potency vs. Efficacy:* Potency is the dose/concentration required to produce 50% of maximum effect ($EC_{50}$); Efficacy is the maximum therapeutic response a drug can produce ($E_{\max}$).
  - *Phase I & II Reactions:* Phase I (Functionalization: oxidation via CYP450, reduction, hydrolysis); Phase II (Conjugation: glucuronidation, sulfation, glutathione conjugation, acetylation).
- **Warfarin:** Oral anticoagulant; competitive inhibitor of **Vitamin K epoxide reductase (VKORC1)**, blocking $\gamma$-carboxylation of clotting factors II, VII, IX, X and proteins C and S. Monitored by PT/INR. Antidote: Vitamin K1 (phytomenadione) and Fresh Frozen Plasma (FFP).
- **Prazosin:** Selective $\alpha_1$-adrenergic receptor blocker; relaxes vascular smooth muscle, decreasing peripheral resistance and BP; relaxes bladder neck and prostatic smooth muscle in Benign Prostatic Hyperplasia (BPH). Adverse effect: First-dose postural hypotension.
- **Chloroquine:** Antimalarial 4-aminoquinoline; accumulates in food vacuole of plasmodium, inhibiting heme polymerase; toxic free heme lyses parasite membrane. Adverse effects: Pruritus, retinal toxicity (bull's eye maculopathy), QT prolongation.
- **Organophosphorus Poisoning:** Irreversible inhibition of acetylcholinesterase $\rightarrow$ accumulation of ACh $\rightarrow$ muscarinic and nicotinic hyperstimulation (SLUDGE: Salivation, Lacrimation, Urination, Defecation, GI distress, Emesis; miosis, bronchospasm, muscle fasciculations). Management: Maintain airway, administer **Atropine** (blocks muscarinic symptoms) and **Pralidoxime (2-PAM)** (cholinesterase reactivator, administered before aging).

### Document 15 — Pathology (End Semester Examination)
- **Septic Shock:** Systemic arterial hypotension and tissue hypoperfusion triggered by overwhelming dysregulated host response to infection (typically Gram-negative endotoxin/LPS or Gram-positive lipoteichoic acid). LPS binds TLR-4 on monocytes, releasing massive TNF-$\alpha$, IL-1, IL-6, causing systemic endothelial activation, widespread vasodilation, capillary leakage, and DIC.
  - *Stages of Shock:* Non-progressive (compensated reflex tachycardia and vasoconstriction), Progressive (tissue hypoperfusion, anaerobic glycolysis, metabolic lactic acidosis), Irreversible (widespread cellular necrosis, multi-organ failure, death).
- **Inflammation & Wound Healing:**
  - *Vascular events:* Transient vasoconstriction $\rightarrow$ persistent vasodilation (hyperemia) $\rightarrow$ increased vascular permeability (exudation) $\rightarrow$ stasis.
  - *Cellular events:* Leukocyte margination $\rightarrow$ rolling (selectins) $\rightarrow$ pavementing/adhesion (integrins: ICAM-1) $\rightarrow$ transmigration/diapedesis (PECAM-1) $\rightarrow$ chemotaxis $\rightarrow$ phagocytosis and microbial killing.
  - *Factors affecting healing:* Infection (most common delay), nutrition (Vitamin C, zinc, protein deficiency), glucocorticoids (inhibit collagen), poor blood supply, diabetes mellitus, mechanical tension.
- **Apoptosis:** Programmed cell death without inflammation. Intrinsic (mitochondrial) pathway initiated by DNA damage/cell stress $\rightarrow$ cytochrome c release $\rightarrow$ binds Apaf-1 $\rightarrow$ activates Caspase-9. Extrinsic (death receptor) pathway initiated by FasL/TNF-$\alpha \rightarrow$ activates Caspase-8. Both converge on executioner caspases (Caspase-3 and Caspase-6) causing nuclear fragmentation, cell shrinkage, and apoptotic body formation.
- **Iron Deficiency Anemia Lab Diagnosis:**
  - *CBC & Blood Film:* Low Hb, microcytic hypochromic RBCs, increased RDW (anisopoikilocytosis), pencil cells/target cells.
  - *Iron Studies:* Low serum iron ($<30\ \mu\text{g/dL}$), markedly elevated Total Iron Binding Capacity (TIBC $>400\ \mu\text{g/dL}$), low transferrin saturation ($<15\%$), markedly reduced serum ferritin ($<15\ \text{ng/mL}$, most specific test).
  - *Bone Marrow:* Absence of stainable hemosiderin iron on Prussian blue reaction.
- **Leukemia & Lymphoma:**
  - *AML Diagnosis:* $>20\%$ myeloblasts in bone marrow/blood; large blasts with Auer rods (pathognomonic, fused peroxidase-positive lysosomal granules); positive myeloperoxidase (MPO) and Sudan Black B staining; flow cytometry reveals CD13, CD33, CD34, CD117.
  - *Hodgkin Lymphoma Morphology:* Characterized by **Reed-Sternberg (RS) cells** (large binucleated/multinucleated giant cells with prominent eosinophilic owl-eye nucleoli, CD15+ and CD30+) set within a non-neoplastic reactive inflammatory background of lymphocytes, histiocytes, plasma cells, and eosinophils. Subtypes: Nodular sclerosis, mixed cellularity, lymphocyte-rich, lymphocyte-depleted.
- **DIC Etiopathogenesis:** Widespread activation of systemic coagulation cascade triggered by release of tissue factor (sepsis, obstetric complications, trauma, acute promyelocytic leukemia) $\rightarrow$ widespread microvascular thrombosis consuming platelets and clotting factors $\rightarrow$ secondary activation of fibrinolysis with release of FDPs/D-dimers $\rightarrow$ paradox of simultaneous widespread thrombosis and severe catastrophic hemorrhage.
