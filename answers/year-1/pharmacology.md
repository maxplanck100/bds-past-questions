## May 20, 2022 — End Semester Examination (50 Marks)

### Section B

**1.** **First-pass metabolism:** The phenomenon whereby a drug absorbed from the GIT undergoes significant metabolism in the gut wall and/or liver before reaching the systemic circulation, reducing its bioavailability. Drugs with high first-pass metabolism (>70%): morphine, propranolol, nitrates (GTN), lidocaine, verapamil, aspirin → require higher oral doses or alternative routes.

**Factors affecting drug absorption:**

| Factor | Effect |
|--------|--------|
| Physicochemical properties: lipid solubility | ↑ lipid solubility → better absorption from GIT |
| Ionisation (pKa + pH) | Unionised form is absorbed (Henderson-Hasselbalch equation) |
| Molecular weight | Small molecules absorbed better |
| Formulation | Tablet → dissolution → absorption; modified release slows absorption |
| Gastric emptying time | ↑ motility → faster absorption; food slows emptying → delayed absorption |
| Intestinal surface area | Larger area (small intestine) → better absorption |
| Blood flow to site | ↑ splanchnic flow → ↑ absorption |
| Drug interactions (chelation, binding) | Antacids + tetracyclines → ↓ absorption (chelation) |
| First-pass metabolism | ↑ first-pass → ↓ bioavailability |
| P-glycoprotein (MDR pump) | Efflux pump; reduces drug absorption |

**2.** **Prodrug:** An inactive or poorly active compound that is converted in the body (usually by enzymatic or chemical reactions) to an active metabolite. Rationale: (i) Improve oral bioavailability. (ii) Reduce first-pass effect. (iii) Improve solubility, stability, or taste. (iv) Target delivery. Examples: Enalapril → Enalaprilat (ACE inhibitor); Codeine → Morphine (CYP2D6); Levodopa → Dopamine (DOPA decarboxylase); Aspirin → Salicylic acid; Cyclophosphamide → Acrolein + alkylating agent; Omeprazole (prodrug activated in acid environment).

**Plasma half-life (t½):** Time taken for plasma drug concentration to fall by 50%. **t½ = 0.693 × Vd / CL** (where Vd = volume of distribution, CL = clearance). **Importance:** (i) Determines dosing interval (in steady state, drug levels stable after 4–5 half-lives). (ii) Predicts time to washout. (iii) Determines time to reach steady state (4–5 t½). Drugs with long t½ (e.g., amiodarone — weeks; warfarin — 40h) have prolonged action and cumulation risk.

**Disadvantages of oral route:**
1. First-pass metabolism reduces bioavailability.
2. Irregular and incomplete absorption (food, pH, motility effects).
3. Onset of action slow — not suitable for emergencies.
4. Cannot be used if patient is unconscious, vomiting, or uncooperative.
5. Irritation to GIT mucosa (e.g., NSAIDs → peptic ulcer).
6. Some drugs destroyed by gastric acid (insulin, penicillin G) or digestive enzymes.

**Essential drug concept:** Essential medicines (WHO concept) are those that satisfy the priority health care needs of the population. Selected based on disease prevalence, clinical efficacy, safety, and cost-effectiveness. The WHO Essential Medicines List (EML) guides national formularies, improving access to affordable, quality-assured medicines, particularly in developing countries. Reduces irrational prescribing.

**Rational drug therapy:** Prescribing the right drug to the right patient, in the right dose, for the right indication, at the right time, at the lowest cost (WHO definition). Reduces adverse effects, drug resistance, and healthcare costs. Principles: (i) Define diagnosis and therapeutic objective. (ii) Select treatment (efficacy + safety profile). (iii) Prescribe clearly. (iv) Inform patient. (v) Monitor treatment. (vi) Stop/adjust treatment.

**3. Short notes:**

**3a. Potency and efficacy with illustration**

**Potency:** Concentration (or dose) of a drug required to produce a given effect (e.g., 50% of maximum response — EC₅₀). A drug with lower EC₅₀ is MORE potent. Potency is a measure of the amount of drug needed.

**Efficacy (Intrinsic efficacy/Emax):** The maximum response a drug can produce, regardless of dose. A drug with high Emax is more efficacious.

*(Draw: dose-response curves on a log scale — two drugs A and B with the same Emax (equal efficacy) but drug A has a lower EC₅₀ (greater potency). Then a third drug C with the same EC₅₀ as B but lower Emax (less efficacious than A or B).)*

**Key distinction:** A more potent drug is not necessarily more efficacious. Aspirin is less potent than morphine for pain (needs a higher dose) but morphine has greater efficacy (can relieve more severe pain).

**3b. Phase I and Phase II metabolic reactions**

**Phase I reactions** ("Functionalisation"): Introduce or reveal a functional group (−OH, −NH₂, −COOH, −SH). Make drug more polar. Mediated mainly by **cytochrome P450 (CYP)** enzymes (CYP3A4, CYP2D6, CYP2C9 most important) in liver. Types: Oxidation (hydroxylation, N-dealkylation, O-dealkylation, S-oxidation), Reduction, Hydrolysis. Phase I product may still be active or inactive.

**Phase II reactions** ("Conjugation/Biosynthetic"): Attach a polar molecule to Phase I product (or to parent drug directly) → water-soluble, inactive, readily excreted. Types: Glucuronidation (UDP-glucuronyltransferases — most common), Sulfation, Acetylation (N-acetyltransferases; fast vs. slow acetylators — isoniazid), Methylation (COMT), Glutathione conjugation, Glycine conjugation.

**3c. Streptokinase**
Thrombolytic (fibrinolytic) drug derived from Group C β-haemolytic streptococci. Mechanism: Binds plasminogen → streptokinase-plasminogen complex → cleaves other plasminogen molecules → active **plasmin** → degrades fibrin clot. **Not fibrin-selective** (degrades systemic fibrinogen also → "lytic state"). Uses: Acute STEMI (within 12h, no PCI available), acute massive pulmonary embolism, acute DVT, acute peripheral arterial thrombosis. Adverse effects: Haemorrhage (major risk), **allergic/anaphylactic reactions** (being a foreign protein), fever. Contraindications: Recent surgery, stroke, active bleeding, severe hypertension.

### Section C

**4.** **Classification of anticoagulant drugs:**

**Parenteral:**
- Heparin (unfractionated — UFH): Potentiates antithrombin III → inactivates thrombin and Xa.
- Low molecular weight heparins (LMWH): Enoxaparin, dalteparin — mainly anti-Xa; more predictable pharmacokinetics; SQ.
- Fondaparinux: Synthetic pentasaccharide, pure anti-Xa.
- Direct thrombin inhibitors: Bivalirudin, argatroban, lepirudin (parenteral).

**Oral:**
- Vitamin K antagonists: **Warfarin** (most common oral anticoagulant traditionally).
- Direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs): Direct thrombin inhibitors — dabigatran; Direct Xa inhibitors — rivaroxaban, apixaban, edoxaban.

**Mechanism of action of Warfarin:**
Warfarin is a **vitamin K antagonist**. It inhibits **VKORC1 (Vitamin K epoxide reductase complex 1)** → prevents regeneration of reduced (active) vitamin K → ↓ γ-carboxylation of glutamate residues of vitamin K-dependent coagulation factors (II, VII, IX, X) and anticoagulant proteins (Protein C, S) → reduced production of functional coagulation factors. **Onset delayed** (4–7 days) — existing factors must be cleared. Factor VII has shortest half-life (~6h) → PT prolonged first. Monitored by **INR (International Normalised Ratio)**.

**Therapeutic uses of Warfarin:** Atrial fibrillation (stroke prevention), deep vein thrombosis (treatment and prophylaxis), pulmonary embolism, mechanical prosthetic heart valves (long-term), antiphospholipid syndrome.

**5.** **Classification of adrenergic drugs by therapeutic effect:**

| Category | Drug examples | Therapeutic uses |
|----------|-------------|----------------|
| α₁-agonists | Phenylephrine, methoxamine | Nasal decongestant, raise BP in spinal hypotension |
| α₁-antagonists | **Prazosin**, terazosin, doxazosin | Hypertension, BPH |
| α₂-agonists | Clonidine, methyldopa | Hypertension (central α₂ → ↓ sympathetic outflow) |
| β₁+β₂-agonists (non-selective) | Isoprenaline | AV block, bronchospasm |
| β₂-agonists (selective) | Salbutamol, terbutaline, salmeterol | Asthma, COPD, preterm labour |
| β-blockers (non-selective) | Propranolol | Hypertension, angina, AF, tremor |
| β₁-selective blockers | Atenolol, metoprolol, bisoprolol | Hypertension, IHD, HF |
| Mixed α+β-blockers | Labetalol, carvedilol | Hypertension (including pregnancy), HF |
| Indirect sympathomimetics | Ephedrine, amphetamine | Narcolepsy, hypotension, nasal decongestant |

**Therapeutic uses of Prazosin:**
1. **Hypertension** (first-line in some protocols; especially with BPH).
2. **Benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH)** — relaxes smooth muscle in prostate and bladder neck (α₁B/D blockade → improve urinary flow).
3. **PTSD (nightmares)** — reduces REM sleep-associated catecholamine response.
4. **Raynaud's phenomenon** — peripheral vasodilation.
5. **Phaeochromocytoma** (pre-operative management, combined with β-blocker).
6. **Chronic heart failure** (adjunct — reduces afterload and preload).

Side effects: **First-dose hypotension** (orthostatic); palpitations, oedema, headache.

**6.** **Mechanism of action of Chloroquine:**

**Mechanism:** Chloroquine (4-aminoquinoline) concentrates in the parasite's food vacuole (acidic) → inhibits haem detoxification. Plasmodium digests haemoglobin → releases toxic free haem → normally polymerised to insoluble, non-toxic **haemozoin** (malaria pigment). Chloroquine binds to **haem** (ferriprotoporphyrin IX) → prevents its polymerisation → accumulation of toxic haem → oxidative damage to parasite membranes → parasite death.

**Additional mechanisms:** May intercalate DNA, inhibit DNA replication and RNA synthesis at high concentrations; lysosomal pH alkalinisation (impairs haem polymerase activity).

**Important therapeutic uses:**
1. Chloroquine-sensitive malaria (treatment and prophylaxis — *P. vivax*, *P. ovale*, *P. malariae*; *P. falciparum* in sensitive areas).
2. **Rheumatoid arthritis** (DMARD — immunomodulatory, lysosomal enzyme inhibition).
3. **Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE)** — reduces flares, organ damage, and mortality.
4. **Extra-intestinal amoebiasis** (amoebic hepatitis, liver abscess — accumulates in liver).

**Adverse effects:**
1. **Retinopathy** (with prolonged use — deposits in RPE → irreversible bull's-eye maculopathy; requires annual ophthalmic screening).
2. **Haemolytic anaemia** in G6PD-deficient patients.
3. Pruritus (especially in dark-skinned Africans).
4. QT prolongation (risk of arrhythmia).
5. Nausea, vomiting (GI).
6. Neurological: Dizziness, headache; rarely, neuropsychiatric effects.

**7. Short notes:**

**7a. Organophosphorus (OP) poisoning and management**

**Mechanism:** OP compounds (e.g., malathion, parathion, nerve agents — sarin, VX) are irreversible inhibitors of **acetylcholinesterase (AChE)** → accumulation of acetylcholine at all cholinergic synapses → cholinergic toxidrome.

**Clinical features (cholinergic excess):**
- **Muscarinic (SLUDGE + Killer B's):** Salivation, Lacrimation, Urination, Defecation, GI cramping, Emesis + Bradycardia, Bronchospasm, Bronchorrhoea.
- **Nicotinic (NMJ + sympathetic):** Muscle fasciculation → weakness → paralysis; tachycardia, hypertension, sweating.
- **CNS:** Anxiety, seizures, coma.

**Management:**
1. **Decontamination:** Remove clothing, wash skin (prevent further absorption); avoid secondary exposure.
2. **Airway, Breathing, Circulation** (support).
3. **Atropine** (muscarinic antagonist): Large doses IV (2–4 mg IV, repeated every 5–10 min until drying of secretions); counteracts muscarinic effects. **No effect on nicotinic (neuromuscular) toxicity.**
4. **Pralidoxime (2-PAM):** AChE reactivator (before "ageing"/irreversible phosphorylation of AChE); must be given **within 24–48 hours**; counteracts nicotinic effects (skeletal muscle weakness).
5. **Benzodiazepines** (diazepam) for seizures.

**7b. Therapeutic uses of Atropine**

Atropine = Competitive, reversible muscarinic antagonist (M₁, M₂, M₃ blockade). Uses:
1. **Bradyarrhythmias** (sinus bradycardia, AV block — blocks M₂ → ↑HR, ↑AV conduction).
2. **OP/carbamate/cholinergic toxicity** (reverses muscarinic effects).
3. **Pre-anaesthetic medication:** Reduces secretions (antisialogogue), prevents laryngospasm, attenuates vagal bradycardia during intubation.
4. **Ophthalmic:** Mydriasis (pupil dilation for fundal examination); cycloplegia (iritis, uveitis — immobilises ciliary muscle).
5. **Antispasmodic:** Irritable bowel syndrome, ureteral/biliary colic (reduces smooth muscle spasm).
6. **Motion sickness / Anti-emetic** (hyoscine — scopolamine, a related drug).

**7c. Therapeutic uses of Propranolol**

Propranolol = Non-selective β₁+β₂-adrenergic receptor antagonist.

1. **Hypertension** — ↓ HR, ↓ contractility → ↓ CO; ↓ renin release.
2. **Angina pectoris** — reduces O₂ demand (↓ HR, ↓ contractility, ↓ wall tension).
3. **Arrhythmias** (AF, atrial flutter, SVT — rate control; Vaughan-Williams Class II antiarrhythmic).
4. **Post-myocardial infarction** — reduces re-infarction, sudden cardiac death.
5. **Heart failure** (with carvedilol/bisoprolol — slow titration, reduces mortality in stable CHF).
6. **Thyrotoxicosis** — controls tachycardia, palpitations; inhibits peripheral T₄ → T₃ conversion.
7. **Migraine prophylaxis** — reduces frequency/severity.
8. **Essential tremor** — β₂ block in peripheral muscle.
9. **Situational anxiety/performance anxiety** (reduces sympathetic symptoms — palpitations, tremor).
10. **Pheochromocytoma** (with α-blocker first to avoid paradoxical hypertension).
11. **Portal hypertension** — reduces splanchnic blood flow → ↓ variceal bleeding risk.

Contraindications: Asthma/COPD (β₂ blockade → bronchoconstriction), bradycardia, heart block, peripheral arterial disease (aggravates Raynaud's).

## March 12, 2022 (New Course, 50 Marks) — Internal Exam

### Section B (Key questions covered above or below)

Additional topics from this paper:

**Receptor types and signal transduction:**
1. **Ionotropic receptors (ligand-gated ion channels):** Fastest. ACh (nicotinic), GABA-A, NMDA (glutamate). Ion flux directly opens upon ligand binding. Onset: milliseconds.
2. **Metabotropic (G protein-coupled receptors, GPCRs):** Coupled to Gs (↑cAMP), Gi (↓cAMP), or Gq (↑IP₃/DAG). Examples: β-adrenergic (Gs), muscarinic M2 (Gi), α₁-adrenergic (Gq). Onset: seconds to minutes.
3. **Kinase-linked receptors (RTKs):** Insulin receptor, EGF receptor → autophosphorylation → kinase cascade. Growth factors.
4. **Nuclear receptors (intracellular):** Steroid hormones, thyroid hormone, vitamin D, retinoic acid → bind intracellularly → regulate gene transcription. Slowest (hours to days).

**Drug tolerance:** Decreased response to a drug after repeated administration, requiring increasing doses to achieve the same effect. Types: Pharmacokinetic tolerance (enzyme induction → increased metabolism, e.g., alcohol tolerance), Pharmacodynamic tolerance (receptor downregulation, e.g., opioids), Tachyphylaxis (rapid tolerance, e.g., nitrates), Behavioural tolerance (adaptation of behaviour).

**Bioavailability:** Fraction of administered drug that reaches the systemic circulation unchanged. For IV = 100% by definition. Oral bioavailability affected by: first-pass metabolism, GI absorption efficiency, formulation.

## March 8, 2021 (50 Marks)

### Section B

**1.** Routes of drug administration and parenteral routes:
- **Oral** (most common; convenient; first-pass; slow onset).
- **Sublingual** (SL): Rapid absorption (under tongue), avoids first-pass (e.g., GTN tablets, buprenorphine).
- **Rectal:** For unconscious/vomiting patients, partly avoids first-pass; erratic absorption.
- **Intravenous (IV):** 100% bioavailability, immediate action; no first-pass; can titrate; risk of infection/air embolism.
- **Intramuscular (IM):** Faster than oral; can give depot injections; not for anticoagulated patients.
- **Subcutaneous (SC):** Slow, sustained absorption; for insulin, LMWH, vaccines.
- **Inhalation:** Rapid onset (asthma inhalers — salbutamol); topical lung delivery; high bioavailability for volatile agents.
- **Transdermal:** Sustained release, avoids first-pass (GTN patch, nicotine patch, fentanyl patch, oestrogen patch); slow onset.
- **Topical/Local:** Direct application to skin/mucosa.

**2.** **Mechanisms of drug action:**
1. **Receptor-mediated:** Agonist (binds receptor → full response), Partial agonist (sub-maximal response even at full receptor occupancy), Antagonist (binds receptor, no response; competitive — reversible; non-competitive — irreversible).
2. **Enzyme inhibition:** Aspirin (COX-1/2), ACE inhibitors, statins (HMG-CoA reductase), carbonic anhydrase inhibitors.
3. **Ion channel modulation:** Local anaesthetics (Na⁺ channel blockade), calcium channel blockers, diazepam (GABA-A Cl⁻ channel allosteric enhancer).
4. **Carrier/transporter:** SSRIs (serotonin reuptake inhibitor), TCA (NA + 5-HT reuptake inhibitor), metformin (MATE transporter).
5. **Physicochemical:** Antacids (H⁺ neutralisation), osmotic diuretics (mannitol), activated charcoal (adsorption).

**3.** **Cholinergic drugs — classification:**
- **Directly acting muscarinic agonists:** Pilocarpine (glaucoma, Sjögren's — oral, activates muscarinic receptors → ↑ secretions, miosis), Carbachol (glaucoma), Methacholine (bronchoconstrictor challenge test), Bethanechol (urinary retention, GI atony).
- **Anticholinesterases (indirect):** (i) Reversible: Neostigmine (myasthenia gravis, post-op paralytic ileus, reverse NMJ block), Physostigmine (Alzheimer's, glaucoma, anticholinergic overdose), Pyridostigmine (MG, DUMBELS — cholinergic symptoms if excess). (ii) Irreversible: OPs and carbamates (see Q7a above).
- **Ganglionic blockers:** Hexamethonium (historical), Mecamylamine.
- **Neuromuscular blockers (nicotinic antagonists):** Depolarising — suxamethonium (succinylcholine; causes fasciculations, then flaccid paralysis); Non-depolarising — pancuronium, vecuronium, atracurium (reversed by neostigmine + atropine).
- **Muscarinic antagonists (anticholinergics):** Atropine, hyoscine, glycopyrrolate, ipratropium (COPD — inhaled), oxybutynin (OAB).

**4. Short notes:**

**4a. Adverse drug reactions (ADR)**

Unexpected, harmful reactions to drugs given at normal therapeutic doses.

**Classification (Rawlins-Thompson):**
- **Type A (Augmented/Predictable):** Extension of pharmacological action; dose-dependent; most common (~80%). Examples: Hypoglycaemia with insulin, bleeding with anticoagulants.
- **Type B (Bizarre/Unpredictable):** Not related to pharmacological action; not dose-dependent; immune-mediated or idiosyncratic. Examples: Penicillin anaphylaxis, halothane hepatitis, G6PD haemolysis.
- **Type C (Chronic/Continuous):** With prolonged use; e.g., adrenal suppression with corticosteroids, osteoporosis, Cushing's.
- **Type D (Delayed):** After discontinuation; e.g., carcinogenesis (alkylating agents), tardive dyskinesia (antipsychotics), teratogenicity.
- **Type E (End-of-use):** Withdrawal effects; e.g., opioid withdrawal, benzodiazepine withdrawal, rebound hypertension.

**4b. General anaesthetics — intravenous agents**

| Drug | Onset | Duration | Uses / Notes |
|------|-------|---------|-------------|
| **Thiopental** (barbiturate) | Rapid (30 s) | Short | Induction of anaesthesia; lowers ICP; absolute overdose causes respiratory depression |
| **Propofol** | Rapid | Short | Induction and TIVA (total IV anaesthesia); smooth, pleasant induction; anti-emetic; rapid recovery; risk: propofol infusion syndrome |
| **Ketamine** (NMDA antagonist) | Rapid | 10–15 min | Dissociative anaesthesia; maintains airway reflexes; bronchodilator; increases BP/HR (good in shock); causes emergence reactions (hallucinations — give diazepam) |
| **Etomidate** | Rapid | Short | Cardiovascular stability; adrenocortical suppression; used in haemodynamically unstable patients |
| **Midazolam** (benzodiazepine) | Moderate | Medium | Anxiolysis, sedation, amnesia; adjunct to anaesthesia |

**4c. Diuretics**

Drugs that increase urine production by reducing renal tubular reabsorption of Na⁺ and water.

| Class | Site of action | Mechanism | Example | Uses |
|-------|--------------|-----------|---------|------|
| Loop diuretics | Thick ascending limb of Loop of Henle | Inhibit Na⁺-K⁺-2Cl⁻ cotransporter (NKCC2) | Furosemide | Acute pulmonary oedema, CCF, hypertension, hypercalcaemia |
| Thiazides | Distal convoluted tubule | Inhibit Na⁺-Cl⁻ cotransporter (NCC) | Hydrochlorothiazide | Hypertension, oedema, hypercalciuria (kidney stones) |
| K⁺-sparing diuretics | Collecting duct | Aldosterone antagonism (spironolactone/eplerenone) or ENaC block (amiloride, triamterene) | Spironolactone | Heart failure, hyperaldosteronism, cirrhotic ascites; used with loop/thiazide to prevent hypokalaemia |
| Carbonic anhydrase inhibitors | PCT | Inhibit CA → ↓ HCO₃⁻ reabsorption | Acetazolamide | Glaucoma, altitude sickness, metabolic alkalosis |
| Osmotic diuretics | PCT + descending loop | Osmotic retention of water in tubule | Mannitol | Raised ICP (cerebral oedema), acute renal failure (preventing oliguria) |

## January 11, 2019 (50 Marks)

### Section B

**1.** **Mechanisms of antibiotic drug resistance:**
1. **Enzymatic inactivation:** β-lactamases (destroy β-lactam ring of penicillins/cephalosporins); aminoglycoside-modifying enzymes (acetyltransferases, nucleotidyltransferases, phosphotransferases).
2. **Altered target site:** Altered PBPs (MRSA — PBP2a encoded by mecA gene → penicillin-resistant); altered 30S/50S ribosomal proteins (aminoglycoside resistance); altered DNA gyrase (fluoroquinolone resistance); altered cell wall precursors (VRE — altered D-Ala-D-Lac instead of D-Ala-D-Ala → vancomycin resistance).
3. **Decreased drug accumulation:** Efflux pumps (MexAB-OprM in *P. aeruginosa*); decreased outer membrane permeability (loss of OmpF porin in Gram-negatives).
4. **Bypass mechanism:** MRSA uses PBP2a which has low affinity for β-lactams; VRE synthesises peptidoglycan with altered terminus.

**Methicillin-resistant *Staphylococcus aureus* (MRSA):** Acquires mecA gene (on mobile SCCmec element) encoding PBP2a → low affinity for all β-lactams → resistant. Treatment: **Vancomycin** (glycopeptide), linezolid, daptomycin, teicoplanin.

**2.** Drug-receptor interactions and dose-response relationship:
- **Full agonist:** High intrinsic efficacy; produces full Emax.
- **Partial agonist:** Produces sub-maximal Emax even at 100% receptor occupancy; acts as functional antagonist in presence of full agonist.
- **Competitive antagonist:** Shifts dose-response curve to the right (↑ EC₅₀) without changing Emax (overcome by increasing agonist dose).
- **Non-competitive (irreversible) antagonist:** Reduces Emax; dose-response curve depressed; cannot be overcome.

*(Draw: dose-response curves on log scale for full agonist, partial agonist, competitive antagonist, and non-competitive antagonist effect.)*

**3.** Opioid analgesics:
- **Classification:** Strong opioids (morphine, oxycodone, fentanyl, methadone), Moderate (codeine, tramadol), Partial agonist (buprenorphine), Mixed agonist-antagonist (pentazocine, nalbuphine), Pure antagonist (naloxone).
- **Mechanism:** Bind µ (mu), κ (kappa), δ (delta) opioid receptors (GPCRs — Gi coupled) → ↓ cAMP → hyperpolarisation → ↓ neuronal excitability → analgesia, euphoria, sedation, respiratory depression.
- **Uses of Morphine:** Severe pain (cancer, MI, post-op), acute pulmonary oedema (↓ preload + sedation), severe diarrhoea (codeine/loperamide preferred in mild cases), cough (antitussive).
- **Adverse effects:** Respiratory depression (most serious), sedation, constipation (no tolerance develops), nausea/vomiting, miosis, urinary retention, physical dependence, pruritus.
- **Tolerance and dependence:** Tolerance (decreased pharmacological effect with repeated dosing, most severe for euphoria and analgesia). Physical dependence: Withdrawal syndrome (anxiety, lacrimation, rhinorrhoea, piloerection, diarrhoea, abdominal cramps, insomnia) on abrupt cessation or naloxone challenge.

**4. Short notes:**

**4a. Pharmacokinetics vs. Pharmacodynamics**

**Pharmacokinetics (PK):** "What the body does to the drug." Describes ADME:
- **A**bsorption (how drug enters body)
- **D**istribution (how drug spreads to tissues; volume of distribution Vd = dose/plasma concentration)
- **M**etabolism (biotransformation, mainly hepatic)
- **E**xcretion (elimination, mainly renal)

**Pharmacodynamics (PD):** "What the drug does to the body." Mechanism of action, dose-response relationship, therapeutic and adverse effects, receptor interactions.

**4b. Drug interactions**
Occur when the effect of one drug is altered by co-administration of another. Types:
- **Pharmacokinetic:** Absorption (antacids + tetracycline → chelation → ↓ absorption), Distribution (displacement from protein binding — warfarin + aspirin), Metabolism (**CYP induction** — rifampicin induces CYP3A4 → ↓ OCP efficacy → pregnancy; **CYP inhibition** — erythromycin inhibits CYP3A4 → ↑ warfarin → bleeding), Excretion (probenecid blocks tubular secretion of penicillin → ↑ penicillin levels).
- **Pharmacodynamic:** Synergism (additive/potentiation — co-amoxiclav; alcohol + sedatives), Antagonism (naloxone reverses opioids).

**4c. NSAIDs**

Non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs. Mechanism: Inhibit **cyclooxygenase (COX-1 and/or COX-2)** enzymes → ↓ prostaglandin synthesis.
- COX-1 (constitutive): Cytoprotective prostaglandins (gastric mucosa), platelet thromboxane A₂.
- COX-2 (inducible): Prostaglandins in inflammation, fever, pain; prostacyclin in endothelium.

**Non-selective (both COX-1 + COX-2):** Aspirin (irreversibly acetylates), Ibuprofen, Naproxen, Indomethacin, Diclofenac, Ketorolac.
**Selective COX-2 inhibitors (Coxibs):** Celecoxib, Etoricoxib — spares gastric mucosa (↓ GI side effects) but ↑ cardiovascular risk.

**Uses:** Pain (headache, dysmenorrhoea, musculoskeletal), fever, anti-inflammatory (RA, OA), low-dose aspirin (antiplatelet — 75–300 mg/day; inhibits TXA₂ in platelets for 7–10 days).

**Adverse effects:** GI (peptic ulceration, GI bleed — especially non-selective), Renal (acute kidney injury — PGs maintain renal blood flow; hypertension, fluid retention), Cardiovascular (↑ MI risk with COX-2 selective and high-dose non-selective; aspirin protects), Hepatotoxicity (diclofenac), Anaphylaxis/aspirin-sensitive asthma (aspirin hypersensitivity — shunting to LOX pathway → ↑ leukotrienes → bronchoconstriction).

## August 13, 2018 (50 Marks)

### Section B

**1.** **Heparin:**
- **Mechanism:** Potentiates **antithrombin III** (increases AT-III affinity for thrombin and Xa 1000×; UFH inhibits both thrombin and Xa; LMWH mainly inhibits Xa).
- **Monitoring:** UFH — aPTT (target 1.5–2.5× control); LMWH — anti-Xa levels (in renal impairment, pregnancy).
- **Antidote:** **Protamine sulphate** (positively charged peptide binds negatively charged heparin → neutralises).
- **Adverse effects:** Haemorrhage, **Heparin-Induced Thrombocytopenia (HIT)** (Type I: early, mild; Type II: immune-mediated IgG anti-PF4-heparin → paradoxical thrombosis — treat with argatroban/fondaparinux), Osteoporosis (long-term), Hyperkalaemia.

**2.** Adrenergic system — α and β receptor effects: (See classified table in Q5 of May 2022 paper above.)

**Adrenaline (Epinephrine) uses:**
1. **Anaphylaxis** — drug of choice (0.5 mg IM, 1:1000); reverses all life-threatening features (α₁ — vasoconstriction, β₂ — bronchodilation, β₁ — ↑HR/contractility).
2. **Cardiac arrest** (1 mg IV, 1:10,000 in ALS — combined effect); restores cardiac rhythm.
3. **Severe asthma (acute)** — SC.
4. **Local anaesthesia adjunct** (vasoconstrictor: prolongs action, reduces bleeding, toxicity — NOT used in digital/penile ring blocks).
5. **Open-angle glaucoma** (topical — reduces aqueous humour production + ↑ drainage).
6. **Haemostasis** (epistaxis — topical application).

**3.** **Oral hypoglycaemic agents (OHAs):**

| Class | Mechanism | Example | Notes |
|-------|----------|---------|-------|
| Biguanides | Activates AMPK → ↓ gluconeogenesis (liver), ↑ insulin sensitivity, ↑ GLUT4 | **Metformin** | Drug of choice for T2DM; no hypoglycaemia; GI SE; contraindicated in renal failure (lactic acidosis risk) |
| Sulphonylureas | Bind SUR1 on β-cells → block K-ATP → depolarisation → ↑ insulin release | Glibenclamide, glipizide | Risk of hypoglycaemia, weight gain |
| Meglitinides | Same mechanism, shorter acting | Repaglinide | Less hypoglycaemia than SUs |
| Thiazolidinediones (TZDs) | PPAR-γ agonist → ↑ insulin sensitivity in adipose/muscle | Pioglitazone | ↑ weight, fluid retention, bladder cancer risk, fracture risk |
| DPP-4 inhibitors (gliptins) | Inhibit DPP-4 → ↑ GLP-1 half-life → ↑ insulin, ↓ glucagon | Sitagliptin, saxagliptin | Weight-neutral; risk of pancreatitis; no hypoglycaemia |
| GLP-1 receptor agonists | Bind GLP-1R → ↑ glucose-dependent insulin secretion, ↓ glucagon, slow gastric emptying, ↓ appetite | Liraglutide, semaglutide | Injectable; ↓ weight; ↓ CV events; nausea |
| SGLT2 inhibitors | Inhibit Na⁺-glucose cotransporter 2 in PCT → ↑ glucosuria | Empagliflozin, dapagliflozin | ↓ CV events, ↓ CKD progression; ↑ DKA risk; genital infections |
| α-glucosidase inhibitors | Inhibit intestinal α-glucosidase → slow CHO digestion → ↓ post-prandial glucose | Acarbose | GI side effects (flatulence, diarrhoea) |

**4. Short notes:**

**4a. Drug metabolism — cytochrome P450**

CYP enzymes (haem-containing mono-oxygenases) are the most important drug-metabolising enzymes; predominantly in liver microsomes; also small intestine, lung. Major isozymes:
- **CYP3A4:** ~50% of drugs metabolised; induced by rifampicin, phenytoin, carbamazepine; inhibited by erythromycin, ketoconazole, grapefruit juice.
- **CYP2D6:** ~25% of drugs; polymorphic (poor vs. extensive metabolisers); metabolises codeine, TCAs, β-blockers, SSRIs.
- **CYP2C9:** Warfarin, NSAIDs, phenytoin; inhibited by fluconazole.
- **CYP1A2:** Caffeine, theophylline, clozapine; induced by smoking.
**CYP induction:** ↑ enzyme synthesis → ↑ drug metabolism → ↓ drug effect; examples: Rifampicin (broad inducer), carbamazepine, phenytoin, alcohol (chronic), St John's Wort.
**CYP inhibition:** Competitive or mechanism-based → ↑ drug levels → toxicity; examples: Ketoconazole, erythromycin, ciprofloxacin, grapefruit juice.

**4c. β-blockers** — (See Q7c, May 2022 paper above for propranolol uses. Additional:)

**Cardioselective β-blockers** (β₁ selective): Atenolol, metoprolol, bisoprolol — preferred in patients with diabetes, COPD, peripheral vascular disease (fewer β₂ side effects). **β-blocker in heart failure:** Counter-intuitive — blocks harmful chronic sympathetic activation → reduces remodelling; improves EF and survival (bisoprolol, carvedilol, metoprolol XL have mortality benefit in HFrEF).

**Antihypertensive drug classes:**

**Antifungal drugs:**

- **Protease inhibitors:** Lopinavir/ritonavir — HIV.
- **Integrase inhibitors:** Raltegravir, dolutegravir — HIV.
- **Aciclovir:** Inhibits viral thymidine kinase → phosphorylated → viral DNA polymerase inhibitor; herpes simplex, herpes zoster.

- **Ribavirin:** Broad-spectrum; HCV (combination therapy), RSV, Lassa fever.
- **Sofosbuvir, ledipasvir, daclatasvir:** Direct-acting antivirals for HCV (NS5B/NS5A inhibitors) — cure rates >95%.

- **Chloroquine:** Mechanism, uses — (See May 2022 Q6 above). Resistance widespread in *P. falciparum*.

## 2015 (September 2015 and January 2016 Papers)

### Key additional topics:

**General anaesthesia — inhalational agents:**
- **Halothane:** Older agent; hepatotoxicity (halothane hepatitis — rare, severe, Type II); cardiac sensitisation (arrhythmias).
- **Isoflurane, desflurane, sevoflurane:** Fluorinated ethers; favoured for modern use. Sevoflurane — low blood/gas partition coefficient → rapid induction/recovery; irritant-free → preferred for induction in children.
- **Nitrous oxide (N₂O):** Weak anaesthetic (cannot produce full anaesthesia alone); analgesic, anxiolytic; "laughing gas"; expanses air-filled spaces — contraindicated in bowel obstruction, pneumothorax, middle ear surgery; associated with B12 inactivation (prolonged use → megaloblastic anaemia).
- **MAC (Minimum Alveolar Concentration):** Concentration in alveolar gas at which 50% of patients do not move in response to a surgical incision; measure of anaesthetic potency. ↓ MAC with: old age, hypothermia, opioids, pregnancy. ↑ MAC with: hyperthermia, alcohol (chronic), stimulants.

**Local anaesthetics:**
- Mechanism: Block voltage-gated Na⁺ channels in the inactivated state from inside the axon → prevent depolarisation → no AP → loss of sensation. Most effective on unmyelinated C fibres (pain) and small myelinated Aδ fibres. Block order: Autonomic > sensory (pain, temp) > sensory (touch, pressure) > motor (last to be blocked).
- **Ester type:** Procaine, cocaine (local), benzocaine (topical) — metabolised by plasma cholinesterase; risk of PABA-related allergy.
- **Amide type:** Lidocaine (lignocaine), bupivacaine, mepivacaine, prilocaine — metabolised by liver CYP enzymes; no PABA allergy.
- **Vasoconstrictor addition (adrenaline):** ↑ duration (vasoconstriction → ↓ systemic absorption), ↓ toxicity, haemostasis. Contraindicated in digital and penile blocks (ischaemia).
- **Bupivacaine:** Longest acting (up to 12h); cardiac toxicity risk (cardiotoxic at toxic blood levels; V-fib — treat with lipid emulsion).
- **Lidocaine (systemic):** Class IB antiarrhythmic; also used for ventricular arrhythmias post-MI.

**Drug toxicity and overdose:**
- **Aspirin (salicylate) toxicity:** Mixed respiratory alkalosis + metabolic acidosis; tinnitus, vomiting, fever; management: gastric lavage (if <1h), activated charcoal, urinary alkalinisation (sodium bicarbonate → ion trapping → ↑ excretion), haemodialysis for severe cases.
- **Paracetamol (acetaminophen) overdose:** Hepatotoxicity (NAPQI metabolite depletes glutathione → hepatocellular damage); treatment: **N-acetylcysteine** (NAC — glutathione precursor; effective within 8h, diminishing benefit up to 24h); activated charcoal if within 1h.
- **Opioid overdose:** Coma, pinpoint pupils, respiratory depression; treatment: **Naloxone** (IV/IM/IN, opioid receptor antagonist — competitive, rapid reversal).
- **Benzodiazepine overdose:** Sedation, respiratory depression (milder than barbiturates); treatment: **Flumazenil** (GABA-A benzodiazepine site competitive antagonist); short-acting — may need repeat doses.
- **TCA (tricyclic antidepressant) overdose:** Anticholinergic features + cardiac toxicity (Na⁺ channel blockade → wide QRS, arrhythmia, hypotension); management: sodium bicarbonate (alkalinisation narrows QRS), supportive.
- **β-blocker overdose:** Bradycardia, heart block, hypotension; treatment: Atropine (for bradycardia), glucagon (increases cAMP independently of β-receptors), high-dose insulin therapy, IV calcium.

**Immunosuppressants:**
- **Corticosteroids (prednisolone, dexamethasone):** Anti-inflammatory and immunosuppressive; used in organ transplantation, autoimmune disease, asthma, ALL, AIHA. Adverse effects: Cushing's syndrome, osteoporosis, diabetes, infections, adrenal suppression.
- **Calcineurin inhibitors:** Ciclosporin, tacrolimus — inhibit calcineurin → ↓ IL-2 → ↓ T-cell proliferation; used in organ transplantation, autoimmune diseases; nephrotoxic.
- **Mycophenolate mofetil:** Inhibits inosine monophosphate dehydrogenase → ↓ purine synthesis → ↓ lymphocyte proliferation; transplant rejection prevention.
- **Sirolimus (Rapamycin):** mTOR inhibitor → ↓ T and B cell proliferation; transplantation.
- **Azathioprine:** Purine analogue antimetabolite → ↓ lymphocyte proliferation; RA, SLE, Crohn's, transplantation.
- **Cyclophosphamide:** Alkylating agent; SLE nephritis, vasculitis, RA, severe autoimmune disease; haemorrhagic cystitis (mesna co-administration).
- **Biologics:** Infliximab/adalimumab (anti-TNF-α — RA, Crohn's), Rituximab (anti-CD20 — RA, lymphoma, AIHA), Abatacept (CTLA4-Ig — RA), Tocilizumab (anti-IL-6R).
