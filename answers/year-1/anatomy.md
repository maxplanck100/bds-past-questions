## 2017 (December 22, 2017 — New Course, 50 Marks)

### Section B

**1. Classify glands and draw a labeled histological diagram of mixed gland. [3+3=6]**

**Classification of glands:**
- By secretion mode: **Merocrine** (exocytosis, e.g., salivary glands), **Apocrine** (apical budding, e.g., mammary gland), **Holocrine** (entire cell secreted, e.g., sebaceous gland).
- By duct presence: Exocrine (with ducts, e.g., pancreas) vs. Endocrine (ductless, e.g., thyroid).
- By secretory product: Serous (watery, enzyme-rich), Mucous (viscid mucin), Mixed (both serous and mucous acini, e.g., submandibular gland).
- By morphology: Simple (unbranched duct) vs. Compound (branched duct); tubular, acinar (alveolar), or tubuloalveolar.

**Histological features of a mixed gland (submandibular):** Serous demilunes (crescents of serous cells) cap the mucous acini. Mucous cells are pale-staining, nuclei flattened basally. Serous cells are darker, pyramid-shaped, nuclei round and basal. Striated ducts modify secretion.

**2. Notochord — formation and fate. [1+2+2=5]**

**Definition:** Notochord is a rod-like mesodermal structure forming the primary axial skeleton of the embryo.

**Formation:** During the 3rd week, epiblast cells migrate through the primitive node in the midline → prechordal plate → notochordal process develops → intercalates into endoderm → notochordal plate → folds into solid notochord by week 4.

**Fate:** (i) Induces overlying ectoderm to form the neural plate (neurulation). (ii) Induces adjacent mesoderm to form vertebrae. (iii) Persists in adults as the **nucleus pulposus** of intervertebral discs. Abnormal persistence/displacement can lead to a **chordoma** (rare midline tumour).

**3. Sesamoid bone — special features and two examples. [3+2=5]**

**Special features:**
- Develop within tendons at points of high friction/compression.
- Protect tendons from wear; alter angle of pull, improving mechanical advantage.
- Outer surface covered by fibrocartilage; inner surface by hyaline cartilage.
- Poorly vascularised; supplied from periosteum and adjacent synovial membrane.
- Do not articulate with other bones directly (exception: patella articulates with femur).

**Examples:** (1) **Patella** — largest sesamoid, within quadriceps tendon, improves extension leverage. (2) **Sesamoid bones in flexor hallucis brevis** — protect long flexor tendon under the 1st metatarsal head.

**4. Short notes:**

**4a. Sympathetic trunk**

The sympathetic trunk (paravertebral chain) is a paired ganglionated cord running on either side of the vertebral column from the base of the skull to the coccyx (where the two trunks unite at the **ganglion impar**). It contains 3 cervical, 11 thoracic, 4–5 lumbar, 4–5 sacral, and 1 coccygeal ganglia. Preganglionic fibres from T1–L2 lateral horn enter via white rami communicantes → synapse in the chain → postganglionic fibres leave via grey rami to spinal nerves or along arteries to target organs. Functions: pupil dilation, heart rate increase, bronchodilation, vasoconstriction, piloerection.

**4b. Thin skin**

Thin skin covers most of the body surface (all except palms and soles). Epidermis has 4 layers (stratum basale, spinosum, granulosum, corneum — stratum lucidum absent). Contains hair follicles, sebaceous glands, and sweat glands. Cornified layer thinner than thick skin. Dermal ridges (papillae) less pronounced.

**4c. Sciatic nerve**

Largest nerve in the body (L4, L5, S1, S2, S3). Exits the pelvis through the greater sciatic foramen **below piriformis**, descends in the posterior thigh between the ischial tuberosity (medial) and greater trochanter (lateral), lying deep to gluteus maximus. Divides at (or above) the apex of the popliteal fossa into **tibial** and **common fibular (peroneal)** nerves. Motor: all hamstrings (except short head of biceps femoris — by common fibular), all muscles below the knee. Sensory: most of the leg and foot. Injury (e.g., posterior hip dislocation, intramuscular injection) causes foot drop, weakness of knee flexion, loss of sensation below knee.

### Section C

**5. Histological features of lung with labeled diagram. [3+2=5]**

**Histological features:**
- **Pleura:** Thin mesothelium over fibroelastic connective tissue.
- **Bronchi:** Cartilage rings (C-shaped), pseudostratified ciliated columnar epithelium, submucosal mucous glands, smooth muscle.
- **Bronchioles:** No cartilage; simple columnar/cuboidal ciliated epithelium; Clara (club) cells secrete surfactant-like substance; smooth muscle prominent.
- **Terminal bronchioles** → **Respiratory bronchioles** (alveolar outpouchings begin here).
- **Alveolar ducts** and **Alveolar sacs.**
- **Alveoli:** Type I pneumocytes (squamous, gas exchange), Type II pneumocytes (cuboidal, secrete surfactant), alveolar macrophages (dust cells).
- Interalveolar septa contain a rich capillary network, elastic fibres, and fibroblasts.

*(Draw: trachea → bronchus → bronchiole → respiratory bronchiole → alveolar duct → alveolus; label epithelium, cartilage, smooth muscle, type I & II pneumocytes, alveolar macrophage.)*

**6. Parts of bronchial tree and normal histological structural changes. [3+2=5]**

**Parts:** Trachea → Primary bronchi (right and left) → Secondary (lobar) bronchi (3 right, 2 left) → Tertiary (segmental) bronchi → Bronchioles → Terminal bronchioles → Respiratory bronchioles → Alveolar ducts → Alveoli.

**Structural changes as air passages narrow:**
- Epithelium: Pseudostratified ciliated columnar → simple columnar → simple cuboidal → squamous (alveoli).
- Cartilage: Rings (trachea) → irregular plates (bronchi) → absent (bronchioles onward).
- Smooth muscle: Increases relative proportion as cartilage decreases; allows bronchomotor tone regulation.
- Goblet cells and mucous glands: Present in bronchi; disappear in bronchioles (replaced by Clara cells).
- Diameter and wall thickness progressively decrease; resistance inversely proportional to 4th power of radius.

**7. Action and nerve supply of muscles of mastication. [3+3=6]**

| Muscle | Action | Nerve Supply |
|--------|--------|--------------|
| Masseter | Elevation (jaw closure), protrusion, lateral movement | Masseteric branch of mandibular nerve (V3) |
| Temporalis | Elevation, retraction, lateral movement | Deep temporal branches of V3 |
| Medial pterygoid | Elevation, protrusion | Medial pterygoid nerve (V3) |
| Lateral pterygoid | Depression (opens jaw), protrusion, lateral movement | Lateral pterygoid nerve (V3) |

All muscles of mastication are supplied by the **mandibular division of the trigeminal nerve (V3)**.

**8. Short notes:**

**8a. Pericardium with its nerve supply**

Pericardium is a fibroserous sac enclosing the heart and roots of great vessels. Layers: (i) **Fibrous pericardium** — tough outer layer, attached to central tendon of diaphragm and blends with vessel adventitia. (ii) **Serous pericardium** — parietal layer (lines fibrous pericardium) and visceral layer (epicardium, covers heart surface); pericardial cavity contains ~50 mL serous fluid, reducing friction. **Nerve supply:** Phrenic nerves (C3,4,5) supply the fibrous and parietal serous pericardium (sensory pain, referred to shoulder). Visceral pericardium: sympathetic and vagal fibres. Pericarditis causes sharp pleuritic chest pain, friction rub; cardiac tamponade in pericardial effusion.

**8b. Paranasal air sinuses**

Air-filled extensions of the nasal cavity within skull bones, lined by respiratory epithelium continuous with nasal mucosa. Four pairs:
1. **Maxillary sinus** — largest; drains into middle meatus (via hiatus semilunaris); floor related to upper molar/premolar roots.
2. **Frontal sinus** — drains into middle meatus via frontonasal duct.
3. **Ethmoid sinuses** (anterior, middle, posterior) — anterior and middle drain into middle meatus; posterior drains into superior meatus.
4. **Sphenoid sinus** — drains into sphenoethmoidal recess.

Functions: lighten skull, resonance for voice, warm/humidify air. Sinusitis most common in maxillary sinus.

**8c. Lacrimal apparatus**

Produces and drains tears. Components: **Lacrimal gland** (superior-lateral orbit; secretomotor supply from facial nerve via greater petrosal → pterygopalatine ganglion → zygomatic → lacrimal nerve) → tears flow medially over conjunctiva → collected at lacrimal papillae into **upper and lower canaliculi** → **lacrimal sac** (in lacrimal fossa) → **nasolacrimal duct** → inferior meatus of nose. Blockage of nasolacrimal duct causes epiphora (overflow of tears).

### Section B

**1. Histological structure of thymus with labeled diagram. [3+3=6]**

Thymus is a bilobed lymphoid organ in the anterior superior mediastinum, most active in childhood and involutes after puberty. **Histology:** Outer fibrous capsule sends **trabeculae** dividing the gland into lobules. Each lobule has:
- **Cortex:** Dense, darkly staining; packed with immature T-lymphocytes (thymocytes) undergoing positive and negative selection; macrophages, dendritic cells, and epithelial reticular cells (form blood-thymus barrier).
- **Medulla:** Pale staining; mature T-lymphocytes, fewer; **Hassall's corpuscles** (concentric whorls of keratinised epithelial cells, unique to thymus; their function is to delete self-reactive T cells via RANK-L expression).

*(Draw: capsule → trabecula → cortex (dark) → medulla (pale) → Hassall's corpuscle.)*

**2. Origin, course and applied anatomy of radial nerve. [3+2=5]**

**Origin:** Posterior cord of brachial plexus (C5, C6, C7, C8, T1).

**Course:** Arises in axilla → spirals in the **spiral (radial) groove** of humerus between medial and lateral heads of triceps with profunda brachii artery → pierces the **lateral intermuscular septum** → enters anterior compartment of arm at the elbow → divides into **superficial** (sensory) and **deep (posterior interosseous)** branches at the level of the lateral epicondyle.

**Applied anatomy:** 
- **Saturday night palsy / crutch palsy:** Compression in axilla → loss of triceps.
- **Spiral groove injury** (mid-shaft humeral fracture): Wrist drop (loss of wrist/finger extensors), triceps spared; loss of sensation over anatomical snuffbox.
- **Posterior interosseous nerve palsy:** Finger drop (wrist extension preserved by ECRL).

**3. Layers of skin with labeled diagram. [3+2=5]**

Skin has two main layers:
**Epidermis (superficial):** Stratified squamous keratinised epithelium. Layers (deep to superficial): Stratum basale (mitotically active, melanocytes, Merkel cells) → stratum spinosum (desmosomes, Langerhans cells) → stratum granulosum (keratohyalin granules) → stratum lucidum (only in thick skin) → stratum corneum (dead keratinocytes, waterproof barrier).

**Dermis (deep):** Connective tissue. Papillary layer (superficial, loose CT, dermal papillae, Meissner's corpuscles) and reticular layer (dense irregular CT, collagen bundles, Pacinian corpuscles, sweat glands, hair follicles, sebaceous glands, arrector pili muscle).

**4. Short notes:**

**4a. Great saphenous vein**

Longest vein in the body. Formed by the dorsal venous arch of the foot, passes anterior to the medial malleolus, ascends the medial leg and thigh, passes through the **saphenous opening** (fossa ovalis) in the deep fascia of the thigh, and drains into the femoral vein approximately 3.75 cm inferolateral to the pubic tubercle. **Tributaries:** dorsal venous arch, accessory saphenous vein; receives several cutaneous tributaries below the saphenous opening. **Clinical:** Varicose veins (incompetent valves); used as graft in CABG and arterial bypass; saphenous cutdown for IV access; long saphenous vein ligation for varicose veins.

**4b. Neurons**

Structural and functional units of the nervous system. **Structure:** Cell body (soma) — contains nucleus, Nissl substance (rough ER + ribosomes), mitochondria, neurofilaments. **Dendrites** — receive signals. **Axon** — single, arises from axon hillock, conducts impulse away; may be myelinated (Schwann cells in PNS, oligodendrocytes in CNS). **Classification:** Unipolar (pseudounipolar, e.g., DRG neurons); Bipolar (retina, cochlea); Multipolar (motor neurons, cerebral cortex). **Functional:** Sensory (afferent), motor (efferent), interneurons.

**4c. Thick skin**

Found only on the palms and soles (and fingertips). Features: Epidermis has all five layers including a prominent **stratum lucidum**; Stratum corneum is very thick. **No hair follicles or sebaceous glands.** Numerous sweat glands. Well-developed dermal ridges (fingerprints). Meissner's (touch) and Pacinian (pressure) corpuscles prominent in dermis. Better withstands mechanical stress.

### Section C

**5. Temporomandibular joint — type, capsular attachments, muscles causing movements. [1+2+4=6]**

**Type:** Synovial, condylar (bicondylar), compound joint with an articular disc.

**Capsular attachments:** Above: Articular tubercle and margin of articular fossa of temporal bone. Below: Neck of mandibular condyle. An articular disc divides the joint into upper (gliding) and lower (hinge) compartments.

**Movements and muscles:**
| Movement | Muscles |
|----------|---------|
| Depression (opening) | Lateral pterygoid (bilaterally), digastric, geniohyoid, mylohyoid (gravity) |
| Elevation (closing) | Masseter, medial pterygoid, temporalis |
| Protrusion | Lateral and medial pterygoids (bilaterally) |
| Retraction | Temporalis (posterior fibres), digastric |
| Lateral (grinding) | Contralateral lateral pterygoid, ipsilateral temporalis |

**6. Nasopharynx — boundary, structures, applied anatomy. [2+2+1=5]**

**Boundaries:** Anteriorly opens into nasal cavity via choanae; floor is soft palate; roof and posterior wall are the base of skull (sphenoid and occipital bones); laterally are the pharyngeal walls.

**Structures present:** (i) Adenoids (pharyngeal tonsil) — on posterior wall. (ii) Eustachian tube opening (pharyngotympanic tube) — opens on lateral wall; guarded by tubal tonsil (Gerlach's tonsil). (iii) Sinus of Morgagni — weak spot near Eustachian tube opening. (iv) Fossa of Rosenmüller (pharyngeal recess) — behind the Eustachian tube opening, common site for nasopharyngeal carcinoma.

**Applied:** Adenoid hypertrophy causes nasal obstruction, mouth breathing, otitis media (blocked Eustachian tube). Nasopharyngeal carcinoma arises commonly from the fossa of Rosenmüller.

**7. Different stages of maturation of lungs. [5]**

Lung development passes through five stages:
1. **Embryonic (4–7 weeks):** Lung bud from foregut endoderm → bronchial tree forms; pulmonary vessels develop.
2. **Pseudoglandular (8–16 weeks):** Branching of bronchi continues; all major airways down to terminal bronchioles formed; resembles exocrine gland histologically; surfactant absent.
3. **Canalicular (17–26 weeks):** Respiratory bronchioles develop; vascular supply expands into future alveolar walls; Type II pneumocytes appear; some surfactant produced.
4. **Saccular/Terminal sac (26–36 weeks):** Terminal air sacs (primitive alveoli) form; thin blood-air barrier; surfactant production increases. Premature infants born at this stage can survive with NICU support.
5. **Alveolar (36 weeks – 8 years):** True alveoli form; secondary septa divide saccules. By age 8, full adult complement (~300 million alveoli) is achieved.

**8. Short notes:**

**8a. Carotid sheath**

A condensation of deep cervical fascia enclosing: **Common and internal carotid artery** (medially), **internal jugular vein** (laterally), **vagus nerve** (posteriorly between artery and vein), deep cervical lymph nodes, and **ansa cervicalis** (on the surface of the sheath). It extends from the base of the skull to the arch of the aorta. Clinically important because carotid artery aneurysm, carotid body tumour (chemodectoma), and surgical access to the carotid vessels all involve this sheath.

**8b. Coronary artery**

Two coronary arteries arise from the **aortic sinuses of Valsalva** just above the aortic valve.
- **Right coronary artery (RCA):** In the right atrioventricular groove → gives off the **right marginal artery** → in 70% (right-dominant) continues as **posterior descending artery (PDA)** supplying the posterior IVS, AV node, and right ventricle.
- **Left coronary artery (LCA):** Short main stem → bifurcates into **Left anterior descending (LAD)** (anterior IVS, LV anterior wall, apex) and **Left circumflex (LCx)** (obtuse marginal branches, left atrium and lateral wall of LV).
- **Dominance:** Determined by which artery gives the PDA (right dominant ~70%, left dominant ~10%, co-dominant ~20%).

**8c. Sternal angle (Angle of Louis)**

Angle formed by the manubrium meeting the body of the sternum at the **manubriosternal joint** (secondary cartilaginous). At approximately the level of **T4/T5** intervertebral disc. Clinically important landmarks at this level: costal cartilage of 2nd rib (reference for counting ribs), arch of aorta begins and ends, trachea bifurcates (carina), ligamentum arteriosum, azygos vein joins SVC, left recurrent laryngeal nerve hooks under aortic arch.

### Section B

**1. Femoral triangle — boundary, contents, applied anatomy. [2+2+2=6]**

**Boundaries:** Superior (base): Inguinal ligament. Lateral: Medial border of sartorius. Medial: Medial border of adductor longus. Floor: Iliopsoas (lateral), pectineus (medial), adductor longus. Roof: Fascia lata (with great saphenous vein piercing it at saphenous opening).

**Contents** (lateral to medial — **NAVY** mnemonic): **N**erve (femoral nerve and branches), **A**rtery (femoral artery), **V**ein (femoral vein), **Y**-lymphatics (deep inguinal lymph nodes) and femoral canal (contains fat, lymphatics).

**Applied:** Femoral hernia descends through femoral canal, appears as a lump below and lateral to pubic tubercle; strangulation common. Femoral triangle is a site for femoral artery access (angiography, IABP). Femoral artery aneurysm pulsatile lump here.

**2. Parts and blood supply of a typical long bone. [1+2+2=5]**

**Parts:** Diaphysis (shaft), Epiphysis (proximal and distal ends, covered by articular cartilage), Metaphysis (growth plate/physis region), Periosteum (outer fibrous + inner osteogenic layer), Medullary cavity (contains marrow), Endosteum (lines medullary cavity).

**Blood supply:** (i) **Nutrient artery** (1–2; main supply; enters diaphysis obliquely, ramifies in medullary cavity). (ii) **Periosteal arteries** (supply outer cortex, important after nutrient artery damage). (iii) **Epiphyseal arteries** (supply epiphysis). (iv) **Metaphyseal arteries.** In children, epiphyseal and metaphyseal supplies are largely separate (physeal barrier), important in haematogenous osteomyelitis.

**3. Histological structure of elastic cartilage. [3+2=5]**

Elastic cartilage (yellow cartilage) is a specialised cartilage retaining flexibility. **Histology:** Matrix contains type II collagen AND abundant **elastic fibres** (visible with Verhoeff-van Gieson stain), which give it resilience. Cells are chondrocytes in lacunae, arranged in groups (isogenous groups). Covered by a perichondrium. Less calcification than hyaline cartilage.

**Examples:** Auricle of the ear, epiglottis, external auditory meatus, corniculate and cuneiform cartilages of larynx, walls of the Eustachian tube.

**4. Short notes:**

**4a. Sympathetic nervous system**

The thoracolumbar division of the autonomic nervous system (preganglionic neurons in lateral horn of T1–L2). Preganglionic fibres (myelinated) → white rami → sympathetic chain ganglia (paravertebral) or prevertebral ganglia (coeliac, superior mesenteric, inferior mesenteric) → postganglionic fibres (unmyelinated) to effectors. Neurotransmitters: ACh (preganglionic), noradrenaline (postganglionic, except sweat glands — ACh). Effects: "Fight or flight" — pupil dilation, tachycardia, bronchodilation, vasoconstriction, decreased GI motility, piloerection, sweating.

**4b. Spermatogenesis**

Process of sperm production in the seminiferous tubules of the testes. Stages: 
1. **Spermatogonia** (2n) → multiply by mitosis; Type A (stem cells) and Type B (committed).
2. **Primary spermatocytes** (2n) undergo **meiosis I** → Secondary spermatocytes (n).
3. **Secondary spermatocytes** → **Meiosis II** → Spermatids (n, round).
4. **Spermiogenesis:** Spermatid → Spermatozoon (acrosome formation from Golgi, flagellum from centriole, nuclear condensation, cytoplasmic stripping by Sertoli cells).
Duration: ~74 days. Supported by **Sertoli cells** (blood-testis barrier, nourishment, FSH receptor, inhibin). Regulated by FSH and LH (Leydig cells → testosterone).

**4c. Palatine tonsil**

Mass of lymphoid tissue in the tonsillar fossa (between the palatoglossal and palatopharyngeal arches). Covered by stratified squamous non-keratinised epithelium (continuous with oropharynx) that dips into crypts (12–20). Separated from pharyngeal muscles by a fibrous capsule. **Blood supply:** Tonsillar branch of facial artery (main), with contributions from lingual, ascending pharyngeal, and lesser palatine arteries. **Nerve supply:** Glossopharyngeal nerve. **Applied:** Tonsillitis, peritonsillar abscess (quinsy — between capsule and superior constrictor), tonsillectomy risk — ligation of tonsillar artery.

### Section C

**5. Development of interatrial septum and congenital anomalies. [3+3=6]**

**Development:**
1. **Septum primum** grows down from the roof of the primitive atrium toward the endocardial cushions, leaving an opening — the **ostium primum**.
2. Ostium primum closes as septum primum fuses with endocardial cushions.
3. Before closure, perforations appear in the upper part of septum primum, forming the **ostium secundum**.
4. **Septum secundum** grows down to the right of septum primum, leaving an opening — the **foramen ovale**.
5. In fetal life: blood flows right → left through foramen ovale (high right atrial pressure). At birth: left atrial pressure rises (pulmonary circulation established) → valve-like closure. Permanent closure (fusion) by ~6 months.

**Congenital anomalies:**
- **Ostium secundum ASD** (most common, ~70%): Defect at the fossa ovalis; septal tissue at the fossa is deficient.
- **Ostium primum ASD** (~20%): Failure of septum primum to fuse with endocardial cushions; associated with AV valve abnormalities.
- **Patent foramen ovale (PFO):** Fails to close (~25–30% of adults); usually haemodynamically insignificant but risk of paradoxical embolism.
- **Sinus venosus ASD:** Near SVC entry; associated with partial anomalous pulmonary venous return.

**6. Layers of deep cervical fascia and investing layer tracing. [1+3+1=5]**

**Layers of deep cervical fascia:**
1. Investing layer (superficial layer of deep cervical fascia)
2. Pretracheal layer (muscular and visceral parts)
3. Prevertebral layer
4. Carotid sheaths

**Investing layer:** Encloses the entire neck like a collar. **Tracing:** From the external occipital protuberance, nuchal ligament, and spine of C7 posteriorly → wraps around to the lower border of the mandible, mastoid process, and superior nuchal line superiorly → continues as the parotid capsule, masseteric fascia, and zygomatic arch superiorly; anteriorly it fuses at the midline and attaches to the hyoid bone; inferiorly it splits to enclose the **sternocleidomastoid** and **trapezius** muscles, and attaches to the manubrium (suprasternal space of Burns), clavicle, acromion, and spine of scapula.

**Applied:** Submandibular space infections can track to the parapharyngeal space; Ludwig's angina involves the investing layer and pretracheal fascia.

**7. Bronchopulmonary segment with applied anatomy. [3+2=5]**

A **bronchopulmonary segment** is the smallest functionally independent unit of the lung, supplied by a tertiary (segmental) bronchus and its accompanying segmental artery, and drained by intersegmental veins. **Structure:** Pyramidal, apex toward the hilum, base at the pleural surface; separated from adjacent segments by intersegmental connective tissue septa.

**Number:** Right lung — 10 segments (3 upper, 2 middle, 5 lower). Left lung — 8–10 (depending on whether apicoposterior and anteromedial are counted together).

**Applied anatomy:** (i) Tumours/infections confined to one segment can be removed by **segmentectomy**, preserving adjacent lung. (ii) **Postural drainage** positions the diseased segment uppermost so gravity aids secretion drainage through the bronchial tree. (iii) Bronchoscopy identifies segmental openings.

### Section A

**1. Histological structure of skeletal muscle. [3+2=5]**

Skeletal (striated voluntary) muscle. **Microscopic structure:** Each fibre (cell) is a syncytium — long, cylindrical, multinucleated (peripheral nuclei). Covered by **sarcolemma** (cell membrane + basement membrane). Inside: parallel **myofibrils** showing cross-striations — alternating **A bands** (dark, myosin + actin overlap) and **I bands** (light, actin only) bisected by **Z discs**; H zone (centre of A band, myosin only); M line (centre of H zone). **Sarcomere** = Z to Z; functional unit of contraction. Abundant **mitochondria** alongside myofibrils. **Sarcoplasmic reticulum** (SR) forms a network around myofibrils; **T-tubules** (transverse) at A-I junctions conduct depolarisation into the cell. **Triad** = 1 T-tubule flanked by 2 SR cisternae.

*(Draw: longitudinal section showing sarcomere, A/I bands, Z disc, H zone, M line, T-tubule, sarcoplasmic reticulum.)*

**2. Classify joints and explain features of synovial joint. [3+1+1=5]**

**Classification:** By tissue uniting bones:
- **Fibrous:** Sutures (skull), gomphosis (teeth in sockets), syndesmosis (tibiofibular).
- **Cartilaginous:** Primary (synchondrosis — epiphyseal plate, 1st rib with manubrium) and Secondary (symphysis — pubic symphysis, IV disc).
- **Synovial:** Most mobile; examples: hip, knee, shoulder.

**Features of synovial joint:** (i) Articular surfaces covered by **hyaline cartilage** (no perichondrium, avascular). (ii) **Joint capsule** — outer fibrous layer + inner synovial membrane. (iii) **Synovial membrane** — secretes synovial fluid (hyaluronic acid + dialysate of plasma; lubricates, nourishes articular cartilage). (iv) **Joint cavity** — potential space containing synovial fluid. (v) Accessory structures: ligaments (intrinsic or extrinsic), bursae, articular discs/menisci, fat pads.

**3. Deltoid muscle — origin, insertion, nerve supply, action. [1+1+1+2=5]**

**Origin:** Lateral third of clavicle (anterior fibre), acromion (middle fibre), spine of scapula (posterior fibre). **Insertion:** Deltoid tuberosity of the humerus. **Nerve supply:** Axillary nerve (C5, C6) — from posterior cord. **Actions:** Anterior fibres — flexion and medial rotation of arm. Middle fibres — **abduction** (main abductor; takes arm from 15° to 90°; supraspinatus initiates 0–15°). Posterior fibres — extension and lateral rotation.

**5. Short notes:**

**5a. Arches of foot**

The foot has three arches maintained by bones, ligaments, and muscles. **(i) Medial longitudinal arch:** Calcaneus, talus, navicular, three cuneiforms, 1st–3rd metatarsals. Keystone: head of talus. Maintained by: spring (plantar caleonavicular) ligament, tibialis posterior, flexor digitorum longus, plantar fascia. **(ii) Lateral longitudinal arch:** Calcaneus, cuboid, 4th–5th metatarsals. Less arched, weight-bearing. **(iii) Transverse arch:** Metatarsal heads and cuboid/cuneiform bases. Functions: shock absorption, rigid lever for propulsion. Flat foot (pes planus) = fallen medial arch; pes cavus = exaggerated arch.

**5b. Hunter's canal (Adductor canal)**

Aponeurotic tunnel in the middle third of the medial thigh. **Boundaries:** Anterior wall: vastoadductor membrane (fascia between vastus medialis and adductors). Posteromedial wall: adductor longus (above) and adductor magnus (below). Anterolateral wall: vastus medialis. **Contents:** Femoral artery, femoral vein (posterior to artery), nerve to vastus medialis, saphenous nerve (exits through roof to become subcutaneous at its lower end). **Applied:** Femoral artery becomes popliteal artery as it passes through the adductor hiatus at the lower end of canal. Adductor canal is the site for femoral arterial bypass surgery.

**5c. Hassall's corpuscles**

Unique to the thymic medulla. Concentric whorls of keratinised, eosinophilic (pink-staining) epithelial reticular cells. Thought to express RANK-L (receptor activator of NF-κB ligand), which drives dendritic cells to induce regulatory T-cell (Treg) development, preventing autoimmunity. They increase in size and number with age. Their presence distinguishes thymus from other lymphoid tissue (useful diagnostically).

**5e. Claw hand**

Characteristic deformity from combined ulnar and median nerve palsy (total claw) or ulnar nerve palsy alone (ulnar claw). **Ulnar claw (ring/little fingers):** Loss of lumbricals → Hyperextension at MCPJs (unopposed long extensors) + Flexion at IPJs (unopposed long flexors) → ring and little fingers clawed. Median nerve adds clawing of index and middle fingers. **More pronounced in ring and little fingers** because those digits' lumbricals are supplied by the ulnar nerve. "Ulnar paradox" — the lower the lesion, the worse the claw because long flexors are intact.

**6. Posterior mediastinum — boundaries, contents, clinical importance. [2+2+1=5]**

**Boundaries:** Anteriorly: Pericardium (below) and trachea (above to carina). Posteriorly: Bodies of T5–T12 vertebrae. Superiorly: Sternal angle (T4/T5 plane). Inferiorly: Diaphragm. Laterally: Parietal pleura.

**7. Formation, tributaries and drainage of coronary sinus. [2+2+1=5]**

**Formation:** Coronary sinus is formed by the union of the **great cardiac vein** and the **oblique vein of left atrium** (Marshall's vein). It lies in the posterior part of the atrioventricular groove.

**Tributaries:** (i) Great cardiac vein (largest). (ii) Middle cardiac vein (posterior IVG). (iii) Small cardiac vein. (iv) Posterior vein of left ventricle. (v) Oblique vein of left atrium (Marshall's vein).

**Drainage:** Into the **right atrium** through the coronary sinus opening, guarded by the **Thebesian valve** (Valve of coronary sinus — a semilunar fold).

**8. Formation and features of lateral wall of nasal cavity. [3+2=5]**

**Formation (embryological):** Lateral wall derives from ethmoid, maxilla, lacrimal, inferior nasal concha, palatine, and sphenoid bones.

**Features:** Three scroll-shaped projections — **superior, middle, and inferior conchae (turbinates)** — project from the lateral wall, increasing surface area. Under each concha is a **meatus**:
- Inferior meatus: Receives nasolacrimal duct.
- Middle meatus: Drains maxillary, anterior ethmoid, and frontal sinuses (hiatus semilunaris, ethmoidal bulla, infundibulum).
- Superior meatus: Receives posterior ethmoid sinuses.
- Sphenoethmoidal recess: Above superior concha; receives sphenoid sinus.

**10a. Azygos lobe**

Developmental anomaly where the azygos vein fails to migrate normally and instead cuts into the upper lobe of the right lung, creating a pleural mesentery. **10c. Valves of heart**

Four cardiac valves: (i) **Mitral (bicuspid):** Left AV, 2 cusps (anterior and posterior), supported by papillary muscles via chordae tendineae. (ii) **Tricuspid:** Right AV, 3 cusps. (iii) **Aortic:** Semilunar, 3 cusps (right, left, posterior), no chordae; contains coronary ostia in right and left sinuses. (iv) **Pulmonary:** Semilunar, 3 cusps (right, left, anterior). The AV valves prevent backflow during ventricular systole; semilunar valves prevent backflow during diastole. Auscultation areas: Mitral — apex, Tricuspid — lower left sternal border, Aortic — right 2nd intercostal space, Pulmonary — left 2nd intercostal space.

**10d. Fallot's tetralogy**

Most common cyanotic congenital heart disease (~10% of all CHD). Four features (all result from **anterior and superior displacement of the infundibular septum**): (1) **Pulmonary stenosis** (infundibular/valvular — main cause of right-to-left shunt). (2) **Overriding aorta** (aorta straddles the VSD). (3) **Ventricular septal defect (VSD)** — large, perimembranous. (4) **Right ventricular hypertrophy** (secondary to outflow obstruction). Clinical: Central cyanosis, clubbing, "Tet spells" (hypercyanotic episodes relieved by squatting), ECG shows RVH. CXR: "Boot-shaped" heart. Treatment: Total surgical correction.

**2. Open and closed circulation of spleen. [2+2+1=5]**

The spleen has a **dual circulation** theory:
- **Open (slow) circulation:** Blood from arterioles is discharged directly into the **cords of Billroth** (splenic cords) in the red pulp → filters through reticular meshwork → enters venous sinusoids → splenic vein. Allows macrophages to remove old/abnormal RBCs. (Supported by older concepts.)
- **Closed (fast) circulation:** Arterial blood enters the venous sinusoids directly via capillaries without entering the cords. Faster but less filtering.
**Current consensus:** Predominantly open circulation. RBCs must deform to squeeze through fenestrations of sinusoidal wall; rigid/old cells are retained and phagocytosed by splenic macrophages. Applied: Splenomegaly in haemolytic anaemia (increased filtration workload).

**4. Short notes:**

**4a. Neurulation**

Process by which the neural plate folds to form the neural tube (3rd–4th week of embryogenesis). Steps: Neural plate (induced by notochord) develops neural folds → folds elevate and fuse at the dorsal midline starting in the cervical region → zipper-like fusion proceeds cranially and caudally → anterior neuropore closes (day 25); posterior neuropore closes (day 27). Neural crest cells (from the margins of neural folds) migrate to form ganglia, pigment cells, adrenal medulla, craniofacial structures. Failure: **Anencephaly** (anterior neuropore fails), **Spina bifida** (posterior neuropore fails). Folic acid supplementation reduces risk.

**4b. Craniosacral nervous system (Parasympathetic)**

The parasympathetic division has preganglionic neurons in two regions: **Cranial outflow** (GVE component of CN III, VII, IX, X) and **Sacral outflow** (lateral horn of S2, S3, S4 — pelvic splanchnic nerves). Long preganglionic fibres synapse in ganglia near or within target organs → short postganglionic fibres. Neurotransmitter: ACh at both synapses. Effects ("rest and digest"): Miosis, bradycardia, bronchoconstriction, increased GI motility, bladder contraction, erection.

**6. Typical intercostal space — what it is, boundaries, contents, clinical importance. [1+1+2+1=5]**
