## PAPER 1 — Oral Biology (First Year, date not printed on scan)

### Section B — 25 marks

#### 1. Describe the different anatomical aspects of Permanent Maxillary Canine? [6]
Permanent Maxillary Canine (Tooth #13 / #23; FDI 13, 23; Universal 6, 11). "Cornerstone of the dental arch":
- **Labial Aspect:**
  - Outline is pentagonal.
  - Distinct single cusp with two sloping ridges: the **mesial cusp ridge is shorter** than the distal cusp ridge.
  - Prominent labial ridge runs vertically from cusp tip to cervical line, dividing the surface into shallow mesiolabial and distolabial developmental depressions.
  - Mesial contact area is at the junction of incisal and middle thirds; distal contact area is more cervical, in the middle of the middle third.
  - Cervical line is convex towards the root apex.
- **Lingual Aspect:**
  - Displays a bulky, prominent **cingulum** in the cervical third.
  - Distinct **lingual ridge** extends from cusp tip to cingulum, flanked by mesial and distal lingual fossae.
  - Mesial and distal marginal ridges are heavy and well developed.
- **Mesial Aspect:**
  - Wedge-shaped (triangular) with a broad labiolingual base at the cervical third, resisting heavy biting forces.
  - Incisal cusp tip is located **labial** to the long axis of the root (diagnostic feature).
  - Cervical line curves incisally by approximately 2.5 mm.
- **Distal Aspect:**
  - Similar to mesial aspect, but cervical line curvature is less pronounced (~1 mm).
  - Distal marginal ridge is thicker and heavier than mesial marginal ridge.
- **Incisal Aspect:**
  - Diamond-shaped / asymmetrical outline.
  - Labiolingual dimension is noticeably greater than the mesiodistal dimension.
  - Cusp tip is situated slightly labial and mesial to the center.
- **Root:**
  - Single, conical, exceptionally long (average 17 mm, longest root in human dentition).
  - Cross-section is ovoid, wider labiolingually.
  - Apical third typically curves distally; developmental depressions present on both mesial and distal root surfaces.

#### 2. Enumerate the morphologic stages of tooth development. Write in detail the events during the Late Bell Stage. [2+3=5]
- **Morphological Stages of Tooth Development:**
  1. Dental Lamina & Bud Stage (Initiation & Proliferation)
  2. Cap Stage (Proliferation & Early Histodifferentiation)
  3. Early Bell Stage (Histodifferentiation & Morphodifferentiation)
  4. Advanced / Late Bell Stage (Apposition of Hard Tissues: Enamel & Dentin)
  5. Root Formation Stage
  6. Eruption and Functional Stage
- **Events during the Late Bell Stage (Appositional Stage):**
  1. *Breakdown of Dental Lamina:* The dental lamina fragments into epithelial islands and cords via mesenchymal invasion. Remnants may persist as the **epithelial rests of Serres**.
  2. *Reversal of Functional Polarity:* Cells of the Inner Enamel Epithelium (IEE) elongate into tall columnar **pre-ameloblasts**. Nuclei migrate away from dental papilla toward the stratum intermedium.
  3. *Odontoblast Differentiation:* Pre-ameloblasts induce adjacent ectomesenchymal cells of the dental papilla to differentiate into columnar **odontoblasts**.
  4. *Dentinogenesis (First Hard Tissue):* Odontoblasts secrete predentin matrix (Type I collagen, phosphophoryn) at the future cusp tips. Mineralization follows as odontoblast processes (Tomes' fibers) elongate.
  5. *Amelogenesis Triggered:* Mineralizing dentin matrix signals pre-ameloblasts to complete differentiation into secretory **ameloblasts**, which develop **Tomes' processes** and deposit enamel matrix over dentin.
  6. *Formation of Cervical Loop:* OEE and IEE meet at the cervical rim to form the cervical loop, the future Hertwig's Epithelial Root Sheath (HERS).
  7. *Establishment of Dentinoenamel Junction (DEJ):* Scalloped boundary between calcifying dentin and enamel is permanently established.

#### 3. Describe the development of mandible in detail. Add note on its clinical considerations? [4+1=5]
- **Development of Mandible:**
  - Develops in the lower portion of the **first pharyngeal (mandibular) arch**.
  - Mainly develops via **intramembranous ossification** around the primary skeletal rod, **Meckel's cartilage** (which acts as a morphogenetic guide but does not directly form bone).
  - *Chronology & Site of Initial Ossification:* Around the 6th week of intrauterine life, ossification begins in the mesenchyme lateral to Meckel's cartilage at the bifurcation of the inferior alveolar nerve into incisive and mental nerves.
  - *Spread of Ossification:* Trabeculae spread anteriorly toward the midline (symphysis menti) and posteriorly along the lateral side of Meckel's cartilage to form the body and ramus.
  - *Fate of Meckel's Cartilage:*
    - Dorsal end ossifies to form the **malleus** and **incus** (middle ear ossicles) and **sphenomandibular ligament**.
    - Intermediate part obliterates; perichondrium forms the anterior ligament of malleus.
    - Ventral part near symphysis contributes minimally to endochondral ossification of the chin (mental ossicles).
  - *Secondary Cartilages (Endochondral Growth):*
    1. **Condylar Cartilage:** Appears at 10–12 weeks; major growth center of mandible up to 20 years of age via endochondral ossification.
    2. **Coronoid Cartilage:** Transient, disappears before birth.
    3. **Symphyseal Cartilage:** Present at mandibular symphysis; fuses by end of 1st year of life.
- **Clinical Considerations:**
  - *Micrognathia / Pierre Robin Sequence:* Mandibular hypoplasia, glossoptosis, and cleft palate due to early intrauterine growth arrest.
  - *Condylar Fractures:* Damage to the vascular condylar cartilage in children leads to severe growth disturbance, mandibular asymmetry, and temporomandibular joint (TMJ) ankylosis.
  - *Agnathia:* Complete failure of mandibular development (rare, fatal).

#### 4. Write short notes on: [3×3=9]
##### a. Dead tracts
- **Definition:** Empty dentinal tubules filled with air or fluid, created when odontoblasts die or their processes rapidly retract due to severe, acute external stimuli (deep dental caries, cavity preparation without cooling, severe attrition/erosion).
- **Microscopic Appearance:** In ground sections viewed under transmitted light, dead tracts appear **black/dark** because the entrapped air scatters light; in reflected light, they appear **white**.
- **Location:** Typically sealed off pulpal-ward by a tertiary (reparative) dentin barrier deposited by secondary odontoblasts to protect the underlying pulp.

##### b. Enamel lamellae
- **Definition:** Thin, leaf-like microscopic defects extending longitudinally from the outer enamel surface inward to the dentinoenamel junction (DEJ) and sometimes into the underlying dentin.
- **Types:**
  - *Type A:* Uncalcified enamel rod segments (developmental hypomineralization).
  - *Type B:* Cracks occurring prior to eruption filled with cellular debris from reduced enamel epithelium.
  - *Type C:* Cracks occurring after eruption filled with organic debris and salivary proteins.
- **Clinical Significance:** Because they are rich in organic material and represent areas of structural weakness, they can serve as microscopic pathways for bacterial invasion and rapid spread of dental caries.

##### c. Dental lamina
- **Definition:** A band of thickened oral ectodermal epithelium appearing around the 6th week of intrauterine life along the future alveolar margins of the maxillary and mandibular arches.
- **Functions & Subdivisions:**
  - *General Dental Lamina:* Gives rise to the 20 primary (deciduous) enamel organs.
  - *Successional (Secondary) Dental Lamina:* Buds lingually from deciduous organs to form 20 succedaneous permanent teeth (incisors, canines, premolars).
  - *Accessory (Accessional) Dental Lamina:* Extends distally to form the 12 non-succedaneous permanent molars.
- **Fate:** Disintegrates by mesenchymal invasion during bell stage. Remnants persist as epithelial rests of Serres, which may give rise to odontogenic cysts, odontomas, or supernumerary teeth.

### Section C — 25 marks

#### 5. Enumerate and add note on the different histological structures of Enamel with well labeled diagram. [5]
- **Histological Structures of Enamel:**
  1. **Enamel Rods (Prisms):** Basic structural units (~5 million in lower incisors to 12 million in upper molars). Sinuous, keyhole- or paddle-shaped in cross section with a cylindrical head directed occlusally/incisally and a tail directed cervically. Hydroxyapatite crystals run parallel to long axis in the head, and fan out obliquely in the tail.
  2. **Striae of Retzius (Incremental Lines):** Represent rhythmic appositional growth and physiological pauses in mineral deposition. In longitudinal sections, appear as dark brownish lines running obliquely from DEJ to surface; on surface appear as shallow grooves called **perikymata**. The **neonatal line** is an exaggerated stria marking birth stress.
  3. **Hunter-Schreger Bands (HSB):** Optical phenomenon seen in longitudinal ground sections under reflected light as alternating light (parazones) and dark (diazenes) bands caused by periodic changes in the 3D direction of adjacent enamel rod bundles. Provides high resistance to vertical masticatory cleavage.
  4. **Gnarled Enamel:** Intricately intertwined and twisted enamel rods situated beneath cusp tips and incisal edges, providing maximum mechanical strength against heavy occlusal impact.
  5. **Dentinoenamel Junction (DEJ):** Distinct scalloped interface with convexities facing dentin, increasing mechanical adhesion between the high-modulus enamel and compliant dentin.
  6. **Hypocalcified Structures:**
     - *Enamel Tufts:* Grass-tuft-like hypomineralized structures projecting from DEJ into inner 1/3 of enamel.
     - *Enamel Lamellae:* Sheet-like organic defects extending from surface to DEJ.
     - *Enamel Spindles:* Trapped odontoblast process ends extending across DEJ into enamel.

#### 6. Define deglutition and explain the stages of deglutition? [1+4=5]
- **Definition:** Deglutition (swallowing) is the complex coordinated physiological process by which a food bolus is transported from the oral cavity through the pharynx and esophagus into the stomach.
- **Stages of Deglutition:**
  1. **Stage 1 — Oral (Voluntary) Stage:**
     - Masticated food is formed into a cohesive bolus on the dorsum of tongue.
     - Tongue tip presses against hard palate behind maxillary incisors.
     - Tongue elevates sequentially from anterior to posterior, squeezing the bolus backward against the palate into the oropharynx through the palatoglossal folds.
  2. **Stage 2 — Pharyngeal (Involuntary) Stage (~1 second):**
     - Initiated when bolus stimulates sensory receptors around the faucial pillars and posterior pharyngeal wall (CN IX, X).
     - *Airway Protection Reflexes:*
       - Soft palate elevates and tightens against posterior pharyngeal wall (Passavant's ridge) to seal off the nasopharynx.
       - Larynx and hyoid bone are pulled upward and forward by suprahyoid muscles.
       - Epiglottis tilts backward over laryngeal inlet; vocal cords and vestibular folds adduct tightly to close glottis.
       - Respiration is temporarily inhibited (deglutition apnea).
     - Pharyngeal constrictor muscles contract in a craniocaudal peristaltic wave.
     - Upper Esophageal Sphincter (cricopharyngeus) relaxes, allowing bolus to enter esophagus.
  3. **Stage 3 — Esophageal (Involuntary) Stage (5–8 seconds):**
     - Cricopharyngeus contracts to prevent reflux.
     - **Primary peristaltic wave** (directed by vagal swallowing center) sweeps bolus down esophagus at 2–4 cm/sec.
     - If bolus lodges, local distension triggers **secondary peristaltic waves**.
     - Lower Esophageal Sphincter (LES) relaxes ahead of bolus via VIP and NO release, allowing passage into gastric cardia.

#### 7. Describe the contents of the dentinal tubules and add a note on intertubular dentin. [3+2=5]
- **Contents of Dentinal Tubules:**
  1. **Odontoblastic Process (Tomes' Fiber):** Cytoplasmic extension of the odontoblast cell body residing in the pulp. Extends through the tubule (proven to occupy inner-to-middle third or full length in young teeth); contains microfilaments, microtubules, mitochondria, and secretory vesicles.
  2. **Dentinal Fluid (Dentinal Liquor):** Extracellular fluid derived from pulpal capillary transudate occupying the **periodontoblastic space** between the odontoblast process and tubule wall. Rich in sodium and protein; fluid movement under capillary/thermal action forms the physical basis of the **Brännström Hydrodynamic Theory of Dentin Hypersensitivity**.
  3. **Nerve Fibers:** Unmyelinated nerve twigs (terminal branches of Plexus of Raschkow) accompanying odontoblast processes in the inner 100–200 μm of coronal tubules, mediating mechanosensory transduction.
  4. **Collagen Fibrils & Non-collagenous Proteins:** Thin, loose matrix proteins in the periodontoblastic space.
- **Intertubular Dentin:**
  - Constitutes the main bulk of circumpulpal dentin, located between the peritubular (intratubular) dentin rings.
  - Composed of a dense, interlocking feltwork of randomly oriented **Type I collagen fibrils** (90% of organic matrix) heavily mineralized with hydroxyapatite crystals deposited parallel to the collagen fibers.
  - Less heavily mineralized than peritubular dentin but significantly more mineralized than mantle dentin. Retains collagen scaffold after acid demineralization.

#### 8. Write short notes on: [3×3=9]
##### a. Saliva
- **Definition & Volume:** Complex, clear, hypotonic biological fluid secreted by major and minor salivary glands (normal daily production: 750–1500 mL; resting pH 6.7–7.4).
- **Composition:** 99.5% water; 0.5% solutes:
  - *Electrolytes:* Na⁺, K⁺, Cl⁻, HCO₃⁻ (primary buffer), Ca²⁺, HPO₄²⁻ (supersaturated, preventing enamel dissolution), thiocyanate.
  - *Organic components:* Salivary amylase (ptyalin), lingual lipase, mucins (MG1, MG2), lysozyme, lactoferrin, histatins, secretory IgA.
- **Functions:** Lubrication and bolus formation; enzymatic digestion; buffering acids (bicarbonate buffer system); antimicrobial defense; remineralization of incipient enamel caries; taste perception (solvent); speech facilitation.

##### b. Biological width
- **Definition:** The combined dimensional height of the **junctional epithelium** and the **supracrestal gingival connective tissue attachment** coronal to the alveolar bone crest.
- **Components & Average Dimensions (Gargiulo et al., 1961):**
  - Connective tissue attachment: ~1.07 mm
  - Junctional epithelium: ~0.97 mm
  - Combined Biological Width: ~**2.04 mm** (often considered 2.0 mm, with an additional ~1.0 mm sulcus depth, totaling 3.0 mm from bone crest to gingival margin).
- **Clinical Significance:** Restorative margins placed within the biological width cause chronic gingival inflammation, attachment loss, pocket formation, and unpredictable alveolar bone resorption. A minimum 3 mm clearance from bone crest to restorative margin is required (achieved via crown lengthening or orthodontic extrusion).

##### c. Alveolar bone proper
- **Definition:** The thin compact bone plate that directly lines the tooth socket (alveolus) into which the principal fibers of the periodontal ligament (Sharpey's fibers) insert.
- **Synonyms & Radiological Features:**
  - *Cribriform Plate:* Anatomical name; perforated by numerous Volkmann's canals transmitting neurovascular bundles between bone marrow and PDL.
  - *Bundle Bone:* Histological name; contains abundant bundles of inserted Sharpey's fibers and is laid down in thin appositional lamellae parallel to the socket wall.
  - *Lamina Dura:* Radiographic term; appears as a continuous, dense, radiopaque white line surrounding the radiolucent PDL space. Disruption or loss of lamina dura indicates periapical pathology, occlusal trauma, or periodontitis.

## PAPERS 2 to 26 — Comprehensive Oral Biology Exam Question Bank

### Section 1: Enamel & Amelogenesis
- **Amelogenesis Stages:** Morphogenic, Organizing/Differentiation, Secretory (Tomes' process), Maturation (ruffle-ended vs smooth-ended cycling), Protective (Reduced Enamel Epithelium), and Desmolytic stage (eruption).
- **Enamel Rods & Architecture:** Keyhole structure; head composed of crystals parallel to rod axis; tail crystals deviate up to 65°. Gnarled enamel under cusps; Hunter-Schreger bands (HSB) prevent vertical crack propagation.
- **Neonatal Line:** Exaggerated incremental line of Retzius found in all primary teeth and permanent first molars, demarcating pre-natal and post-natal enamel apposition.
- **Hypomineralized Structures:** Tufts (inner 1/3, rich in tuftelin), Spindles (odontoblast process entrapment, hypersensitive), Lamellae (transverse cracks/faults, caries pathways).

### Section 2: Dentin & Pulp Complex
- **Dentinogenesis:** Odontoblasts secrete predentin rich in Type I collagen and phosphophoryn; matrix vesicle mineralization in mantle dentin; globular vs linear mineralization in circumpulpal dentin.
- **Types of Dentin:**
  - *Primary Dentin:* Formed up to functional tooth eruption; includes mantle dentin (outer 20–150 μm, first formed, less mineralized, von Korff's fibers) and circumpulpal dentin (main bulk).
  - *Secondary Dentin:* Formed after root completion and tooth eruption throughout life; deposited slowly at pulpal border; uneven deposition leads to progressive reduction of pulp chamber size.
  - *Tertiary (Reparative/Reactionary) Dentin:* Formed focal to localized external insults (caries, attrition, trauma); reactionary (by surviving primary odontoblasts) vs reparative (by newly recruited odontoblast-like cells from pulpal stem cells).
  - *Sclerotic (Transparent) Dentin:* Dentinal tubules obliterated by progressive deposition of peritubular dentin; refractive index matches matrix, appearing translucent in ground sections.
- **Pulp Histology:**
  - 4 Zones: (1) Odontoblast layer, (2) Cell-free zone of Weil, (3) Cell-rich zone (fibroblasts, undifferentiated mesenchymal cells), (4) Pulp core (rich vascular and nerve plexus).
  - Functions: Formative, Nutritive, Sensory (A-delta for sharp localized dentinal pain; C-fibers for throbbing dull pulpitis pain), Protective/Defensive (tertiary dentin, inflammation).
  - Age changes: Decreased vascularity and cellularity, increased fibrosis, decreased pulp volume (due to secondary/tertiary dentin), formation of true/false pulp stones (denticles).

### Section 3: Periodontium (PDL, Cementum, Alveolar Bone, Gingiva)
- **Periodontal Ligament (PDL):** Specialized fibrous connective tissue ~0.15–0.38 mm wide (narrowest at middle third of root, acting as fulcrum).
  - *Principal Fiber Groups (Alveolodental):*
    1. Alveolar crest fibers: Resists extrusion and lateral forces.
    2. Horizontal fibers: Resists horizontal/lateral forces.
    3. Oblique fibers: Most numerous group; runs obliquely from cementum coronally to bone; translates vertical masticatory pressure into tensile stress on bone.
    4. Apical fibers: Radiates from root apex; resists extrusion and protects apical vessels.
    5. Interradicular fibers: In furcation of multi-rooted teeth; resists torque and tipping.
  - *Gingival Fiber Groups:* Dentogingival, Alveologingival, Circular, Dentoperiosteal, Transseptal (connects adjacent teeth across interdental septum; responsible for orthodontic relapse).
  - *Cells:* Fibroblasts (most numerous, rapid turnover), osteoblasts, osteoclasts, cementoblasts, odontoclasts, undifferentiated mesenchymal cells, **Epithelial rests of Malassez** (remnants of HERS, origin of radicular cysts).
- **Cementum:** Avascular, non-innervated mesenchymal hard tissue:
  - *Primary (Acellular extrinsic fiber cementum - AEFC):* Cervical 2/3 of root; composed of inserted Sharpey's fibers; primary role in tooth anchorage.
  - *Secondary (Cellular intrinsic fiber cementum - CIFC):* Apical 1/3 and furcations; contains cementocytes in lacunae; rapid apposition for functional compensation and repair.
  - *CEJ Relationships (Choquet's Rule):* Cementum overlaps enamel (60–65%), Edge-to-edge abutment (30%), Gap with exposed root dentin (5–10%, leads to cervical dentin hypersensitivity).
- **Alveolar Bone:** Consists of Alveolar Bone Proper (cribriform plate / bundle bone / lamina dura) and Supporting Bone (buccal/lingual cortical plates and intervening trabecular spongiosa). Resorbs after tooth extraction.
- **Dentogingival Junction & Junctional Epithelium (JE):** Non-keratinized, collar-shaped epithelium attached directly to tooth enamel/cementum via internal basal lamina and **hemidesmosomes**. Highly permeable with wide intercellular spaces; permits egress of Gingival Crevicular Fluid (GCF) and PMN migration; forms primary barrier against periodontal pathogens.

### Section 4: Oral Mucosa & Salivary Glands
- **Classification of Oral Mucosa:**
  1. *Masticatory Mucosa:* Hard palate and attached gingiva; orthokeratinized or parakeratinized stratified squamous epithelium; dense collagenous lamina propria bound to periosteum (mucoperiosteum).
  2. *Lining Mucosa:* Lips, cheeks, floor of mouth, ventral tongue, soft palate; non-keratinized stratified squamous; loose lamina propria and elastic submucosa allowing distensibility.
  3. *Specialized Mucosa:* Dorsum of tongue; contains lingual papillae and taste buds.
- **Tongue Papillae:**
  - *Filiform:* Most numerous, velvet appearance, cone-shaped, keratinized surface, **no taste buds**; purely mechanical/tactile.
  - *Fungiform:* Mushroom-shaped, scattered among filiform papillae, red vascular appearance, contain taste buds on apical surface.
  - *Circumvallate (Vallate):* 8–12 large papillae arranged in a V-shaped row anterior to sulcus terminalis; surrounded by a circular moat (trench); taste buds in lateral walls; **glands of von Ebner** (purely serous) flush secretions into the moat.
  - *Foliate:* Leaf-like folds on posterolateral tongue margins; contain lateral taste buds.
- **Salivary Glands:**
  - Parotid (pure serous; Stensen's duct opposite maxillary 2nd molar).
  - Submandibular (mixed ~80% serous, 20% mucous with serous demilunes; Wharton's duct opens at sublingual papilla).
  - Sublingual (mixed, predominantly mucous ~70%; Bartholin's duct and ducts of Rivinus).
  - Ductal system: Intercalated duct (simple cuboidal) $\rightarrow$ Striated duct (mitochondria-packed basal infoldings for Na⁺ active reabsorption and K⁺ secretion) $\rightarrow$ Excretory duct (pseudostratified to stratified columnar).

### Section 5: Dental Anatomy & Morphology
- **Permanent Maxillary First Molar (#16, #26):**
  - Largest tooth in maxillary arch. Rhomboidal occlusal outline.
  - 4 major cusps (Mesiobuccal, Distobuccal, Mesiopalatal, Distopalatal) + 1 supplemental cusp (**Cusp of Carabelli** on mesiopalatal cusp).
  - Order of cusp size: Mesiopalatal > Mesiobuccal > Distobuccal > Distopalatal > Carabelli.
  - **Oblique Ridge:** Prominent ridge crossing occlusal surface obliquely from distobuccal cusp to mesiopalatal cusp.
  - 3 divergent roots: Palatal (largest, roundest, longest), Mesiobuccal (broad buccolingually, often 2 canals: MB1 and MB2), Distobuccal (smallest, conical).
- **Permanent Mandibular First Molar (#36, #46):**
  - Pentagonal occlusal outline; trapezoidal from buccal.
  - 5 cusps: 3 buccal (Mesiobuccal, Distobuccal, Distal) and 2 lingual (Mesiolingual, Distolingual).
  - Two roots: Mesial (broad, kidney-shaped, 2 canals) and Distal (oval, 1 or 2 canals).
  - Occlusal grooves form a characteristic **W-pattern** or cross configuration with central, mesiobuccal, and distobuccal developmental grooves.
- **Theories of Tooth Eruption:**
  1. *Root Formation Theory:* Growing root pushes crown coronally (disproven as teeth with severed roots or rootless teeth still erupt).
  2. *Periodontal Ligament Traction Theory (Most Supported):* Fibroblasts (myofibroblasts) in the PDL possess contractile properties, cross-linked collagen contraction pulls tooth toward oral cavity.
  3. *Vascular / Hydrostatic Pressure Theory:* Hydrostatic fluid pressure within apical dental tissues elevates tooth.
  4. *Bone Remodeling Theory:* Selective coronal bone resorption (osteoclasts) and apical bone deposition (osteoblasts) coordinated by the dental follicle.
